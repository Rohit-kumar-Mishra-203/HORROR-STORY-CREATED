Title - The Mirror in the Locked Room
Every family that lived in the farmhouse outside Limerick between and knew that the room on the second floor was locked and that the key was gone. They assumed the previous tenants had lost it. None of them forced the lock because the house had more rooms than they needed and a locked room in an old Irish farmhouse was simply one of the ordinary mysteries of old Irish farmhouses, like the wells that had been filled in and the hearths that had been bricked over and the trees at the property boundary that had been planted in configurations nobody now alive could explain.
James Creery broke the lock in , not from curiosity but from practicality: he had a third child arriving and needed the room. He forced it with a chisel on a Tuesday afternoon in early spring. The room was empty of everything except dust and one object: a mirror, standing upright in the center of the room on a small wooden base, free-standing and balanced. Not leaning against any wall. Not secured to the floor. Simply standing in the center of the dustless he would note this later, when his wife asked him to describe it carefully, before he had decided that careful description was not something he wanted to engage in the center of the room, while everything else was thick with the accumulation of thirty years of closure, this one object was spotlessly, completely, impossibly clean.
He looked at himself in the mirror. He was there. His reflection was precise and immediate and correctly synchronized. He raised his hand. His reflection raised its hand. He turned his head. His reflection turned its head. Everything was correct. Everything was normal.
The room behind his reflection was not the room he was standing in.
The reflected room was darker than the room he occupied, lit by a source he couldn't identify, its proportions slightly wrong in a way he couldn't precisely name the ceiling a few inches lower, perhaps, the window set at a different angle, the floorboards running in a different direction. And the reflected window, which in the real room showed the overcast Limerick sky and the fields beyond, showed in the mirror only a flat, uniform black. Not night sky. Not a storm. A blackness that had no depth and no source and that had apparently been there long enough to be normal.
His reflection looked directly at him. This was correct. Reflections look at you. But his reflection's expression was not his expression. His expression was the careful blankness of a man trying not to react to something. His reflection's expression was engaged. Focused. His reflection opened its mouth. He had not opened his mouth. His reflection moved its lips in words he could not hear through the glass, deliberately, with the steady pace of someone communicating something important to someone on the other side of a window, someone they need to be sure understands. His reflection's eyes were the same color as his eyes. They were oriented directly at him. They were not his eyes.
He left the room. He closed the door. He told his wife that the room was not usable. She asked him why. He said the floorboards were unsafe. He bought a new padlock at the hardware store in town that afternoon and installed it on the door and put the key in his pocket and kept it there for six years until they moved, and when they moved he left the key hanging on its hook in the kitchen and did not take it with him.
The family that moved in after the Creerys found the room locked with a padlock. The padlock was not the one James had installed. It was older the kind that had not been manufactured since before the war. No one had a key. No one broke the lock. They were, they said, the kind of family that did not need all its rooms.
