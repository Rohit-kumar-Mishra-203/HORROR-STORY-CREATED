Title - The Music Box of Harrow Street
The music box was found inside a wall.
This is where most of these stories begin not with a purchase or an inheritance, but with a wall opened for reasons of necessity, renovation or repair, and something found inside that had no business being there. The workers who broke through the plaster of the condemned tenement on Harrow Street, Edinburgh, in the autumn of were not expecting anything. They found a cavity behind the master bedroom wall, roughly the size of a small suitcase, sealed on all sides with original plaster that had never been disturbed. Inside the cavity, wrapped in oilcloth that had rotted to threads, was a music box of dark lacquered wood, approximately the size of a shoebox, with a brass winding mechanism on one side and a small silver latch on the other. The oilcloth had decomposed. The music box had not. The lacquer was intact. The brass was tarnished but unrusted. And it was playing.
This is the detail the workers would return to repeatedly in the years following, when asked about it at pubs or by curious relatives, when the story surfaced at dinner tables and they found themselves compelled to tell it again despite not being able to explain it satisfactorily. The music box had been sealed in a cavity inside a wall that had not been opened in at minimum forty years, probably longer. There was no mechanism by which it could have been wound. The spring inside any music box will eventually exhaust its tension. No spring, left alone in a sealed wall cavity for forty years, retains enough energy to produce sound. And yet it was playing when they broke through. A single melody, eight notes, repeating. Thin and reedy. Wrong in a way none of them could name, the way something is wrong in a dream when you cannot identify the specific feature that signals unreality but your body knows before your mind does.
Douglas Parrish, the foreman, took it home. He had a habit of collecting odd things, old things, objects with histories he would never fully know. His wife Moira tolerated this habit with the patience of someone who has learned which battles to fight. She listened to the melody that first evening and said it was pretty, in a hollow sort of way, and went to bed. Douglas stayed up and listened to it wind down around midnight, the notes slowing, stretching, becoming something lower and stranger before stopping entirely.
The next morning, the melody had changed.
Moira heard it first. She was making tea at six in the morning, early riser, and the music box was on the kitchen table where Douglas had left it, and it was playing something different. Not the eight-note sequence they had heard the day before. Something longer, more complex, with a structure that moved through variations the way a real composition did, building and retreating and building again. She stood in the kitchen holding an empty mug and listened and understood, with a certainty she could not rationally justify, that the melody had a subject. That it was about something. That it was, specifically, about a person. And that the person it was about was close enough for the music to find.
Douglas's decline began in the second week. Sleep disturbances first waking in the night with no memory of the hours between sleep and waking, finding himself in rooms he hadn't consciously entered, standing before objects he hadn't meant to approach. He was found three times by Moira standing before the music box in the kitchen at hours between two and four in the morning, eyes open, expression absent, hands at his sides, listening. He retained no memory of these episodes. He went to his doctor, who found nothing physically wrong and suggested stress. Douglas accepted this diagnosis because the alternative required him to articulate something he did not have words for.
The scratches appeared in the third week. Shallow lines on the insides of both forearms, not painful, not deep enough to bleed significantly, but deliberate in their arrangement. A diagram of overlapping circles connected by straight lines, repeated on both arms with a consistency of detail that suggested the same hand making the same marks from memory rather than a single frenzied episode. Douglas said he had not made them. He said this with the particular flatness of someone who was not sure whether they were lying. Moira photographed the marks and brought the photographs to a retired professor of occult history at the University of Edinburgh, a man named Dr. Archibald Mure, who had spent forty years studying the intersection of folklore and documented historical strangeness.
Dr. Mure looked at the photographs for a long time without speaking. Then he asked Moira three questions: whether the music box was still in the house, whether it was still playing, and whether Douglas had been alone with it at any point during the episodes she had described. When she answered yes to all three, he told her to bring the music box to him immediately, sealed in a bag, without listening to it on the journey. He was not dismissive. He was not condescending. He spoke with the careful gravity of someone communicating urgent information to a person who does not yet understand the full weight of what they are hearing.
She brought it. He locked it in a cabinet in his study, gave her a copy of his key, and said that the cabinet was not to be opened without her present. He would not explain further. He said he needed time to research, and that she should keep Douglas away from any music-producing objects in the interim, and that if the melody appeared anywhere else in dreams, in ambient sound, in the patterns of other noises she was to call him immediately.
He died eight weeks later. Cardiac arrest, sudden, in his office at the university. He was forty-four. He had been, by all accounts, in excellent health. His death was not suspicious in any formal sense, only in the informal sense of the word, only in the way that certain deaths feel like they have been arranged rather than occurred.
When Moira retrieved the cabinet, the music box was gone. In its place was a single sheet of paper. On the paper, scratched in an implement she could not identify, was the diagram from Douglas's arms. Exactly the same. Down to the proportions. The cabinet had been locked with her key and Dr. Mure's key and no other. Both keys were accounted for. There was no explanation. There is still no explanation.
Douglas recovered, partially, over the following year. He was never again entirely the person he had been. He did not sleep well for the remainder of his life. He kept the kitchen light on at all hours. He would not discuss the music box, but there were mornings when Moira found him sitting at the kitchen table before dawn with his hands flat on the surface and his head tilted slightly, as though listening to something she could not hear.
In , sixteen years later, residents of a building two streets from Harrow Street reported a melody coming from an empty flat on the third floor. It was described as thin, reedy, wrong in a way nobody could name. By the time anyone located the building manager and obtained entry, the flat was silent and locked from the inside with the deadbolt thrown, and no one was found. The flat had been vacant for eleven months. There was no music box. There was no mechanism of any kind that could have produced sound. There was only, on the kitchen table, a faint ring in the dust where something approximately the size of a shoebox had rested for what the building manager estimated, based on the undisturbed accumulation of dust around the ring, was a very long time.
