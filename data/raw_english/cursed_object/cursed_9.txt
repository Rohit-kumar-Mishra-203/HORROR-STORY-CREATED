Title - The Bell
The bell was cast in for a church in the Ore Mountains of Saxony by a bell-founder whose name appears in the foundry records but has been obscured by water damage. The foundry records survive in a German regional archive. They contain an unusual notation beside the bell's entry: a small symbol in the margin, hand-drawn, not part of any standard foundry notation system, that has been interpreted by historians as a warning marker of some kind, though what it warned of and who added it are unknown.
The church used the bell for one hundred and forty-two years. In , the church council voted to remove it and the minutes of that meeting have been lost, which may be coincidence. The notation in the surviving ecclesiastical records says only that the bell was to be stored unrung. Not melted down. Not sold. Stored, specifically unrung, in a barn on the property of a local landowner named Von Hess, which is where it remained until , when Von Hess's estate was broken up and an English collector named Aldous Frame purchased it as part of a job lot of European antiquities, not entirely sure what he was buying.
Frame brought it to his estate in Somerset and placed it in the garden as an ornament. For seventy-eight years it sat in the garden and was used as a planter and as a conversational object for guests and as a perch for the estate's cats. In a groundskeeper named William Ash caught it with a rake while trimming the lawn around it. It rang once. The estate's housekeeper, who was in the kitchen forty yards away with the window closed, heard it through the stone walls and the closed window and the distance, heard it as clearly as though the bell were beside her, and the sound went through her chest and into her teeth and into a place she later described as behind her thoughts, a physical place she had not previously known she had.
That night, seven people on the estate woke to the sound of the bell ringing. The estate's owner, Aldous Frame's grandson Reginald, counted the rings in his notes. Seven. Evenly spaced. Coming from the garden where no one was. The next night, the same. On the third night, someone struck the bell deliberately and it rang eleven times from a single strike, the sound expanding rather than decaying, growing fuller and deeper as it continued, filling the grounds and moving past them into the surrounding countryside, where farms a mile away reported hearing a bell at an hour when no bell should ring.
Reginald Frame was found the following morning seated in the garden facing the bell. He was not unconscious. He was simply not responding. He sat in the dew-wet grass in his nightclothes facing the bell with his hands on his knees and his eyes open and did not respond to his name or to touch. By afternoon he had recovered. He could not account for the hours between going to bed and being found. He said only that he had been listening to something and that the something had a great deal to say and that he had not understood it and that he did not expect to understand it and that it was continuing.
The tolling he heard from that point was internal and private a single toll, distant, irregular, audible to him alone, at intervals that followed no pattern he or anyone else could discern. He described it as patient. As though the bell on the other end of the sound had a great deal of time and was not signaling urgency so much as continuity, not distress so much as the simple ongoing fact of its existence, ringing because ringing was what it was and the distance between them was not, from the bell's position, a particular obstacle.
The bell was buried in at the instruction of Reginald's doctor, who was grasping at something practical to do. The tolling in Reginald's ears did not stop. He died in and his final journal entries, which his family preserved and which have been seen by several researchers, describe the sound as having changed in his last weeks. Not louder. Not more frequent. Closer. As though the distance between him and whatever was ringing had finally, after twelve years, begun to close.
What is buried in the garden of the former Frame estate cannot be located. The current owners have tried. The ground-penetrating equipment used in showed nothing in the area where the burial was recorded. Either the bell is not where the records say it was buried, or it is somewhere below the resolution of the equipment used, or it is not there in any sense that the equipment can detect.
