Title - The Painting That Would Not Burn
Catherine Meade received the painting in the autumn of , posted from the estate of her uncle Desmond, a man she had met twice as a child and both times found deeply unsettling in a manner she was too young to articulate and too old to dismiss. Desmond had died intestate, and the estate had been divided by a solicitor with no particular knowledge of or interest in its contents, and the painting had been addressed to Catherine because her name appeared on a list in Desmond's study beside a brief description of the object that the solicitor found confusing and chose not to include in his correspondence. Catherine would later discover the description. It read: for the one who will understand what it requires.
The painting was an oil portrait, approximately three feet by four feet, in a dark wooden frame. It depicted a woman in a black dress standing in a doorway. The setting was ambiguous interior or exterior, impossible to determine, the light sourceless and flat. The woman's face was turned slightly away from the painter, oriented toward something outside the frame, her expression unreadable from the visible angle. Catherine hung it in the parlor, on the wall opposite the window, and thought nothing particular of it for five days.
On the sixth day, her daughter Brigid, who was seven, refused to enter the parlor. She could not explain why. She was not a fearful child, not prone to avoidance or anxious behavior. She simply stood at the parlor threshold and would not cross it, and when pressed she cried and said the woman in the picture watched her, and that the woman's watching was different from the watching of other painted figures, that it moved, that it followed, that it was aware. Catherine thought her daughter was being imaginative and said so. She did not change her assessment for another week, when she noticed that the woman in the painting was standing differently than she had been.
She could not prove this with photographs. She had none from the first days. She knew it, though, with the certainty one has about physical objects that live in familiar space, the way you know whether a piece of furniture has been moved even if you cannot articulate the specific evidence. The angle of the shoulder was different. The placement of the hand on the doorframe was different. And the face, still turned partially away, had rotated by what she estimated as ten degrees further toward the viewer. Not facing her. Not yet. But less averted.
Her husband Patrick burned it in October. He built a fire in the backyard, placed the painting in it, and stood watching until the canvas had blackened and curled and the frame had charred and the fire had consumed what the fire consumes. He was satisfied. He went to bed satisfied. He was not satisfied in the morning.
The painting hung in the parlor. Intact. The frame unmarked by fire. The canvas undamaged. The painted woman facing forward now, directly, her expression visible for the first time and impossible to characterize precisely not malevolent, not benign, not empty. Focused. With the focused quality of something that has been waiting for a specific event and has now confirmed that the event is occurring on schedule.
They burned it again. They buried the ashes in three separate locations. In the morning, the painting hung in the parlor. They removed it from the house and left it at the edge of the property. In the morning, the painting hung in the parlor. Patrick wrote to three priests and received two responses, both politely skeptical and ultimately useless. The third priest, Father Colm Derry of the parish of Saint Brendan, came to the house, examined the painting, spent thirty minutes alone in the parlor with the door closed, and left without speaking to either Catherine or Patrick. He walked to his bicycle at the gate and stopped with his hand on the handlebar and stood there for a moment with his back to the house. Then he got on his bicycle and rode away. He transferred to a rural parish in Galway within the month, a move that surprised his congregation and that he described in correspondence as "a call to simpler work." He died in and his papers were donated to the diocesan archive. His private journals from the years and are catalogued but have been marked, in pencil, in a handwriting that does not match the archivist's notation system, as not for general access.
Catherine stopped writing about the painting in her letters after November of . The letter in which the account is most detailed ends mid-sentence, as though the writing was interrupted suddenly and the writer did not return to it. The letter was addressed to her sister in Limerick. Her sister reported never receiving it. It was found in , tucked into the back of the painting's frame, still sealed, still unfinished, when a subsequent owner attempted to have it restored. The restorer, a man with twenty years of professional experience, worked for one afternoon on the painting and then returned his fee by post, without explanation, and retired. He moved to a village in Clare and reportedly never entered a room containing paintings again, which is an extraordinary limitation to place on one's life and suggests an extraordinary reason for doing so.
The painting remains in the house. The current owners do not discuss it. They have declined three requests from researchers to examine it. They have declined to sell it. One of them, when asked by a journalist why they would not sell a painting that reportedly caused such distress, said simply that they did not think selling it would help. That the painting would go where it needed to go regardless. That it always had.
