Title - The Rocking Chair
No one in the Alcott family ever sat in the rocking chair by choice. It had been the grandmother's, Agnes Alcott, who had spent the last eleven years of her life in it by the fireplace in the corner of the sitting room, who had been found in it on the morning of her death, upright, eyes open, hands folded in her lap, the chair still moving in the slow pendulum arc of a person who has just finished sitting and whose weight has only lately departed. The family kept it because families keep the chairs of the dead. Because the chair is still a chair and chairs are useful. Because throwing it out would be an act of deliberate severance from something that the family was not, in aggregate, ready to sever from.
The self-rocking began six weeks after Agnes's death. Elspeth Alcott, who was eight and an insomniac, heard it first the rhythmic creak of the chair's runners on the floorboards, slow and even and purposeful, the sound it had made for eleven years when Agnes was alive and in it. She lay in her bed and listened and thought, in the rational way that eight-year-olds can be rational before the world has taught them how much energy it takes to maintain that rationality, that it must be a draft, or the house settling, or some acoustic property of old buildings at night. She thought this for three nights before she got up and went to look.
The chair was rocking with the rhythm of a person who is comfortable and at rest. Not the rapid rocking of agitation. Not the slow irregular motion of a draft. The particular cadence of a person who has been in their chair for hours and has settled into the rocking as a background condition, automatic, continuous, the body maintaining motion without conscious direction. The cushion and this is the detail Elspeth would return to for the rest of her life, the detail she told only once, to a therapist, when she was in her forties and had begun to understand that some things need to be said aloud at least once before they can be properly filed away the cushion was depressed. Shaped. Bearing the specific indentation of a person's weight, distributed across the seat in the particular pattern of someone seated for a long time in the same position. The chair was rocking. The cushion held the shape of someone sitting in it. The room was empty.
Then the shaped indentation in the cushion turned toward her.
She had not known a shape could turn. The weight redistribution was subtle, a shifting of the cushion's depression from one geometry to another, the chair's arc adjusting by a few degrees to accommodate a new orientation. But the result was unmistakable: the empty weight of whoever was sitting in the rocking chair was now facing the doorway where Elspeth stood in her nightgown with her hand on the frame. The rocking continued for a moment. Then it slowed. Then it stopped. The chair was still. The cushion held its shape. The room was silent.
Elspeth went back to bed and lay on her back and stared at the ceiling and did not cry, which surprised her later when she thought about it. She should have cried. She was eight and she had seen something that could not be explained and she was alone in the dark with it. She did not cry. She lay very still and breathed very carefully and waited for morning, and in the morning the chair was in its corner and the cushion was smooth and no one had sat in it.
The chair stayed in the family for thirty years. It rocked on its own always between midnight and three, always when no one was in the room, though twice Elspeth's mother reported seeing it from the hallway and both times she said nothing about it to anyone. The indentation appeared and disappeared. The chair was never warm, which Elspeth noted scientifically when she was twelve and old enough to be scientific, but it was never cold either, in a room that in winter was always cold. The chair maintained a neutral temperature independent of the room around it. She wrote this down. She kept records. She grew up and moved away and when her parents died she inherited the chair and put it in her own sitting room and watched it and kept records for another decade and eventually concluded that the chair was not haunted in the way the word implies not violent, not malevolent, not communicative. That whatever occupied it was simply continuing to do what it had always done, which was to sit in the chair by the fire in the corner of the room and rock and find comfort in the rocking and require nothing from anyone in the room except that they not sit in the chair, which the family had never done anyway.
She donated it to a museum of local history in . The museum accepted it, catalogued it, and placed it in a storage room. Three staff members resigned within the first year, citing unease with the storage area. The chair is not on display. It remains in storage. A museum volunteer who visited the storage room in to retrieve an unrelated item reported that the chair was rocking when she entered and stopped when she turned to look at it directly, and that the cushion was shaped as though someone had only just stood up. She submitted a written account of this to the museum director. The director filed it. When contacted by a researcher fifteen years later, the director said she remembered the incident but had decided at the time not to investigate further, and that she stood by that decision.
