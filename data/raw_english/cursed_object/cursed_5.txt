Title - The Doll With Glass Eyes
The family in Bruges who donated the doll to the antique shop in refused to touch it during the donation. The shopkeeper, a man named Hendricks who had run the shop for twenty years and was not given to dramatic interpretation of ordinary events, noted this in his record book because it struck him as genuinely unusual. Families donating items to antique shops were sometimes sentimental about the objects, sometimes indifferent, sometimes eager to be rid of them. He had never had a family stand at a distance from an object they were giving away, unwilling to make contact with it, one child crying silently throughout the transaction, the adults communicating via gesture that Hendricks should handle the object himself.
The doll was Victorian, porcelain face and hands, cloth body, approximately eighteen inches tall. The craftsmanship was excellent the kind of work done by makers who treated dolls as serious objects deserving serious attention. The glass eyes were amber, an unusual color for a doll of that period, and had a quality that Hendricks noted in his record book as unusual depth. He meant this technically the eyes appeared to have a three-dimensional quality that standard glass doll eyes, which are painted spheres or simple glass hemispheres, do not typically have. They appeared to go back into the head further than they should. They appeared to have an interior. When the light in the shop shifted, which it did throughout the day as the sun moved and the quality of the illumination changed, the amber deepened and lightened in ways that tracked the light with more specificity than glass should.
He put it in the window display, priced at forty francs. Three customers expressed interest. None completed a purchase. The first customer changed her mind without giving a reason, setting the doll down on the counter with the particular care of someone setting down an object they have decided they were wrong to pick up. The second told Hendricks, flatly and without elaboration, that the doll had moved its head while she was examining it. The third simply put it on the counter and left quickly. Hendricks called after her. She did not respond. She did not slow down.
He moved the doll to a locked display cabinet in the back of the shop. The following morning it was in the window display. He was certain he had locked the cabinet. He examined the lock. It showed no evidence of tampering. He moved the doll back to the cabinet and watched it lock. The following morning it was in the window again, this time oriented outward toward the street with its amber eyes facing the pedestrians outside. He brought a locksmith, who confirmed the lock was functioning correctly and had not been forced. The locksmith did not look at the doll while he worked. Hendricks noticed this. The locksmith did not mention it.
The priest who came, Father Van der Haag, was in the shop for forty minutes. Hendricks waited in the street. When the priest came out he was walking faster than he had arrived. He said the prayers had been said and that Hendricks should keep the doll in the locked cabinet and sell the cabinet along with the doll as a unit and disclose its history fully to any buyer. He did not say what he had experienced inside. He transferred to a parish in Antwerp shortly after. Hendricks attempted to contact him twice by letter. The letters were not answered.
Hendricks donated the doll to the museum in Ghent in because he had been unable to sell it, unable to dispose of it, unable to have it removed from his shop without it returning, and had decided that a museum's institutional weight its cataloguing, its documentation, its locked storage might constitute a form of containment that his cabinet could not. The museum accepted it. The acquisition is recorded in the ledger in standard archival notation. The doll is not in the museum's current records. It is not in the building. No one who currently works at the museum can recall it. Three staff members who worked there in the s and can still be contacted have no memory of a doll matching the description. The ledger entry exists. Beside it, in handwriting that does not match any other notation in the book, in ink that has a brown undertone that standard archival ink does not have, someone has written two words: still watching.
The handwriting has been dated by a forensic document examiner who was shown the ledger without context. She estimated the ink to be between thirty and fifty years old, consistent with the s to s. She noted that the pen pressure was heavier than the surrounding entries, as though the writer had been pressing down hard, or as though the hand that wrote it was not entirely steady.
