Title - The Pocket Watch of the Fenwick Family
The watch stopped the night Harold Fenwick died. This was November , and Harold's son Gerald, who inherited the watch along with a modest amount of debt and a house that needed its roof replaced, noted the stopping but attributed it to neglect his father had been ill for months, and a watch left unwound will stop. He wound it. It ran for exactly six minutes and stopped at : . He wound it again. Six minutes, then : . He brought it to a watchmaker on the high street, a meticulous Glaswegian named Mr. Torrance, who examined the mechanism for an hour and found nothing wrong. The spring was intact. The escapement was functional. The gears were properly aligned. Mr. Torrance set the watch to noon and placed it on his workbench and sat with Gerald and they watched it together and at six minutes past noon it stopped and the hands read : .
Mr. Torrance asked Gerald to take it away. Gerald did not press for an explanation and Mr. Torrance did not offer one. They understood each other, in the manner of two pragmatic men encountering something neither was equipped to pragmatically process.
The watch became a fact of the Fenwick household over the following decade. Gerald wore it daily, wound it daily, noted daily that it stopped at : after six minutes of running, and said nothing about this to anyone except his wife Margaret, who accepted it with the equanimity of a woman who had been married long enough to know that some things simply are what they are. Gerald replaced the movement entirely in new parts, sourced from a different watchmaker in a different city, no connection to the original mechanism. The watch stopped at : , six minutes after being set, on the day he received it back. He wore it for the rest of his life. The watch stopped. Every day. At : . After six minutes.
Gerald's son Thomas inherited it in . Thomas was an engineer, a rationalist, constitutionally resistant to conclusions that could not be supported by evidence. He wore the watch and documented its behavior in a notebook with the rigor of a man who believed that sufficiently careful observation would eventually yield an explanation. He had three notebooks by the end of the first year. He had never worn a watch more reliably than this one. It stopped every day at : . He began to find this, despite himself, comforting a regularity so absolute it became a kind of anchor. The watch did what it did. The world was strange and the watch was strange and at least the watch was consistent.
He began to wonder, in his third notebook, what : meant. Not mechanically. Mechanically it meant nothing the watch did not know the time it was displaying any more than a broken clock knows it's right twice a day. But the watch was not broken. Every watchmaker who examined it confirmed the mechanism was sound. The watch was choosing, if one was willing to use that word, which Thomas was not, not in writing, to stop at a specific time each day after a specific interval. He began researching the deaths of Fenwick family members going back as far as records allowed. He found that his grandfather, his great-aunt, and his father's brother had all died at times recorded as approximately : , either in the morning or at night. He found one record that specified p.m. He found two that did not specify. He found one, his grandfather's, that said exactly : p.m. in the handwriting of a night nurse who had been precise in all her other notations.
Thomas wrote in his third notebook: it is not measuring time. I do not know what it is measuring. Then he crossed it out. Then he wrote it again on the next page and did not cross it out.
He died in March . His daughter Claire found him in the morning, in his bed, peacefully, as the expression goes, though Claire would later say she had always found the expression dishonest that death in a bed is not peaceful in the way that a field is peaceful, that there is a stillness in a room after death that is not the stillness of peace but the stillness of absence, total and particular and nothing at all like rest. The watch was on his bedside table. Claire, who had grown up knowing the watch's history but had never particularly believed in it, picked it up without thinking. It was running. She checked her phone. It was : . She watched the watch advance. She watched it reach : . It stopped.
She drove to a reservoir early the following morning before anyone was awake and threw the watch as far out over the water as she could. The next day the death notice in the local paper, which had been submitted by the funeral home based on the time noted by the attending doctor who had certified the death, listed the time of Thomas Fenwick's passing as : p.m.
Claire has not owned a watch since. She checks the time on her phone. She will not say whether she looks at it at : . She will not discuss the watch with anyone who knew her father. She will say only, when pressed, that she sometimes thinks the watch was not malfunctioning. That it was doing exactly what it was designed to do. That she does not know who designed it that way or what they intended the design to serve, and that this is the version of the story she finds most bearable to live with.
