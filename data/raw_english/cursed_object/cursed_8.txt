Title - The Typewriter
The literary magazine in Prague was small and serious and published work that the editor, a man named Frantisek Hora, believed had the quality of necessary strangeness fiction that recognized the gap between the world as it is understood and the world as it actually operates, and that was willing to work in that gap without trying to close it. He received a submission in from a contributor named only K. Silber, an address in the old town, three stories of approximately three thousand words each. He accepted all three without hesitation. He wrote to K. Silber at the address provided. The letter was returned. The address did not exist. He published the stories anyway, and when the contributor's copies were returned to him by the postal service, marked undeliverable, he kept them on his desk and wrote a second letter to the same address, which was also returned, and then he stopped writing letters and simply filed the copies and moved on.
The typewriter arrived a month after the third story was published. It was left in the front office of the magazine with no accompanying note, addressed simply to the magazine. Frantisek assumed it was a donation, which occasionally happened writers and readers sometimes contributed equipment. He put it in the back room and forgot about it for two weeks until his archive assistant, a woman named Eva Bloch, began using it for correspondence.
She told him about the additional lines after the third week. She was precise and unemotional in her account, which was her characteristic mode: when she left for the day and returned the next morning, there were additional lines at the end of whatever she had been composing. Always at the end. Always three lines. Always in the same typeface as the rest of the document, indistinguishable in weight or impression from the lines she herself had typed. The content varied. Sometimes it was a sentence describing weather, or a fragment of dialogue between unnamed speakers. Sometimes it was more specific. On one occasion it described, in accurate detail, a conversation Eva had had with her sister the previous evening in her own kitchen.
Frantisek tested this himself. He typed a letter, noted the final line, covered the typewriter and locked the room. The next morning there were three additional lines. They described something he recognized only two days later, when it occurred: an argument he would have on that Thursday with a supplier, including specific words he would say and specific words the supplier would say and the outcome, which was the outcome he experienced. He read his own words back to himself afterward and could not determine whether he had said them because the lines were accurate or whether the lines were accurate because he had read them. He thought about this for a long time and was unable to resolve it.
He moved the typewriter to a storage room. Additional lines appeared on documents in the storage room that had not been brought near the typewriter. He moved the typewriter to a building across the street. Additional lines appeared on documents in his own apartment. He burned the typewriter in April of . The additional lines appeared on documents in his possession for the four months following the burning, declining gradually in frequency, four or five a week becoming one or two, becoming occasional, becoming once, becoming silence.
The silence was worse than the lines. He wrote this in a private journal that survived the war and was discovered in an archive in . The journal entry reads: When it was writing to me, there was a relationship. It knew where I was. It knew what I would do and what I would say and it told me, in its way, that it was attending. The silence now is not peace. It is attention without communication. It is being watched by something that has stopped talking but has not stopped looking.
