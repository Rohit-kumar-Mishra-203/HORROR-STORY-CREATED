The last house on the road had been empty for twenty years. Everyone in the area knew it. Everyone avoided it.

A development company bought it. They sent a team to assess the property.

The team went in on a Monday morning. By Monday afternoon three of the five had left. Different reasons. Sudden illness. Family emergency. One who simply said I have to go and drove away without collecting her equipment.

The remaining two completed the assessment.

Their report described the property as structurally sound. Good bones. No major issues.

It also contained a section at the end that the company's lawyers later said was unprecedented in their experience of property assessments.

The two surveyors had written together, in both their handwriting alternating line by line, a description of something they said they had both seen simultaneously in the basement.

The description was detailed. Clinical. Written in the flat professional language of a property report.

What they described was not flat or clinical. What they described should not have been in a basement.

The lawyers removed that section before filing the report.

The development company sold the property at a significant loss.

Neither surveyor would discuss what they had written.

But they had stayed in contact. They met for coffee every few months. They never explained why.
