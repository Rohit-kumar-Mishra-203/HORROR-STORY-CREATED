THE PAINTING THAT CHANGED

The painting arrived at the museum on a Monday in January, and by the following Friday, two members of the curatorial staff had resigned without notice, the security guard who monitored Gallery had requested a permanent transfer to the north wing, and the director, Dr. Helena Crane, had begun keeping the lights in Gallery at maximum brightness at all hours, which was against protocol and which she could not, when pressed, satisfactorily explain.

The painting was called "Descent" and was attributed to an anonymous Flemish painter of the early seventeenth century. It depicted a landscape a flat, darkened plain under a sky that occupied most of the canvas, a sky of extraordinary complexity, its cloud formations rendered with a technical mastery that had initially excited the acquisition committee. The figure in the painting was small, positioned in the lower left, walking away from the viewer into the landscape. The figure was carrying something what, exactly, was unclear, the detail too small and too obscured by the surrounding darkness of the paint to identify with certainty. The overall composition was sinister in mood but not explicitly disturbing. The acquisition committee had acquired it for a modest sum from a private estate in Belgium and were pleased with themselves.

Dr. Crane had had reservations from the beginning that she had not fully articulated. She was a rigorous woman and she did not like having reservations she couldn't articulate, so she had suppressed them. She was beginning to regret this.

The first problem was the figure. On the day of installation, the figure was in the lower left of the canvas. By Wednesday, when young conservator James Whitfield happened to compare his installation photographs with the painting as it hung, the figure was measurably closer to the centre of the canvas. Not dramatically an inch, perhaps two. But the installation photographs were precise and James was precise, and the discrepancy was there.

He brought this to Dr. Crane, who looked at the photographs and the painting for a long time and then said: "Vibration from the installation process. The paint may have shifted on the canvas." This was not, James knew, how paintings worked. He said nothing because Dr. Crane's face was performing a kind of determined neutrality that he recognised as the face of someone who needs to believe what they are saying.

The second problem was what the figure was carrying.

This was more difficult to document because it was a matter of perception rather than measurement. James had looked at the painting enough times to feel confident in his initial reading: the figure was carrying what appeared to be a bundle cloth, perhaps, or an indistinct shape consistent with multiple possibilities. By Thursday, what the figure was carrying looked different. It was not a bundle. The shape had clarified had become more specific, more detailed in a way that was inconsistent with the painting having simply hung on the wall for four days. It looked now like a person. A small person, inert, carried over the figure's shoulder like a sack.

James showed this to his colleague Rachel, who had also worked with the painting during installation. Rachel looked at it for a long time without speaking. Then she said: "I need some air," and left the gallery, and returned the next morning with a resignation letter.

The third problem was what the painting did to the gallery.

Gallery was a large, well-lit room with eight other works in it all reputable, all thoroughly documented, all without unusual histories. None of them changed. But the room itself changed, in ways that the remaining staff member assigned to Gallery a security guard named Paul Archer documented in the incident reports he filed every evening, with increasing emotional investment and decreasing composure. He reported that the room was colder than the rest of the museum, and that this coldness was specifically concentrated near "Descent." He reported sounds not sounds from the painting, but sounds from the edges of the room, from the corners, from the space between the painting and the adjacent wall. He described these sounds as "like something trying to get comfortable in a space that is too small for it." He reported that visitors to Gallery consistently spent less time there than in any other gallery, and that they left with expressions he described as "bothered not scared exactly, bothered, like when you can't remember if you locked the door."

He reported one further thing, in his final incident report before requesting his transfer: that on Thursday evening, after closing, while he was doing his rounds, he had looked at the painting from the doorway of Gallery and had seen the figure turn.

Not fully. Not dramatically. A partial rotation the figure, which faced away from the viewer into the painted landscape, had turned approximately thirty degrees toward the viewer. Its face was not visible; it had not turned far enough for that. But the angle of the head, the set of the shoulders, were now directed not entirely away but partially toward the room.

Paul Archer had stood in the doorway for a long time. Then he had walked to the far end of the museum and sat down on a bench near the Greek antiquities and stayed there until his shift ended.

Dr. Helena Crane commissioned an independent authentication and conservation assessment. The assessor a respected academic named Dr. Frank Osei spent two days with the painting and produced a report that was technically meticulous and that contained, in its penultimate paragraph, a sentence that Dr. Crane read several times: "This painting contains technical anomalies that are inconsistent with conventional seventeenth-century Flemish technique, including evidence of layering in the central figure that suggests the image has been modified at some point subsequent to its original creation. The nature and purpose of these modifications are outside the scope of standard conservation analysis."

Dr. Crane called the estate in Belgium. The solicitor who handled the estate was cooperative but unhelpful: the painting had been in the family for two hundred years and there were no records of its earlier provenance. It had always been kept in a back room of the house, covered. No family member had liked it. They had been, the solicitor admitted with professional neutrality, relieved to sell it.

"Why did they keep it covered?" Dr. Crane asked.

"The family felt," the solicitor said, carefully, "that looking at it for extended periods was inadvisable."

Dr. Crane returned the painting to the estate. She cited conservation concerns. The museum's acquisition committee was displeased. She did not explain further.

The painting went back to Belgium. The estate, two months later, donated it to a religious institution a monastery in rural France, whose prior accepted it with what the solicitor described as "unusual specific enthusiasm."

The prior placed it in the monastery's deepest room, in the oldest part of the building, facing the wall. He placed certain things around it. He conducted certain prayers over it. He did this with the calm confidence of someone dealing with a known quantity, a categorised problem.

The figure in the painting is facing the wall now. This is, the prior would tell you if you asked, the correct direction for it.

He checks, once a week, to make sure it has not turned around.

So far, it has not.
