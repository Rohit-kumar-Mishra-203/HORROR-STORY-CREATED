When Callum Voss inherited his grandmother's house, he inherited everything in it the furniture, the forty years of accumulated possessions, the three rooms of books, and the thing in the basement that his grandmother had spent sixty years keeping contained.

He did not know about the last item initially. Nobody had told him. His grandmother, Edda Voss a woman of great intelligence and considerable secrecy had kept the knowledge to herself for her entire adult life, and she had died unexpectedly of a stroke at eighty-seven without having made the arrangements she had apparently intended to make. There were notes. Callum found them eventually, in a locked box in her bedroom that he broke open with a kitchen knife six weeks after moving in, when he had begun to understand that something was in the basement and that understanding it was more urgent than respecting his grandmother's privacy.

Edda Voss's notes were meticulous. She had kept them for sixty years a journal, in effect, though she never called it that. They described in precise, unaffected prose what was in the basement of her house, how it had come to be there, and what she did about it.

The story, as Callum pieced it together from the notes and from additional research, was this:

In , Edda's father Callum's great-grandfather, a man named Heinrich Voss who had been a scholar of some distinction and some notoriety had conducted a ritual in the basement of the house that was his then and that he had built specifically to his specifications, with a basement whose dimensions and materials were chosen with obsessive care. The ritual had been his life's work. He had spent thirty years preparing for it. He was attempting, the notes implied without stating directly, to compel a specific entity into a specific kind of agreement to bind it to a contract that would benefit his family in exchange for terms that the notes described only as "provisions of accommodation."

Heinrich Voss had succeeded in part. He had made contact. He had established a presence in the basement. He had not, however, completed the binding had died of a heart attack at the ritual's critical moment, leaving the entity present and the contract unsigned.

Edda had been seventeen. She had found her father in the basement and had understood, from his papers and from the immediate evidence of her own senses, what he had attempted and how it had been left. She had spent the next sixty years managing what he left behind.

The management, as she described it in her notes, was straightforward in concept and demanding in practice. The entity in the basement was present, was conscious, was capable of exerting influence on the house and its inhabitants. The influence was limited Heinrich's preparatory work, while incomplete, had established a structure that constrained it. But the constraint was maintained by attention: weekly rituals, specific materials replenished on a schedule, certain words spoken in a certain order on certain days of the month.

"It is not malign in a simple sense," Edda wrote in a note dated . "It does not seek to destroy or harm. It seeks to complete what father began to finalise an agreement that would, from its perspective, be mutually beneficial. The problem is that I have read father's terms carefully, and what it considers mutually beneficial is not what I consider mutually beneficial. The 'provisions of accommodation' involve more than I am willing to provide. So we are at an impasse: it pushes at the edges of its constraint, and I maintain the constraint, and this has been our relationship for thirty-eight years."

The notes in the final decade of Edda's life became increasingly concerned with succession. She had no children. Callum her brother's grandson, the nearest family member had been her intended inheritor of the knowledge as well as the house, but she had procrastinated in the telling of it, reluctant to burden him, uncertain of his readiness, and then the stroke had come.

"If you are reading this without having been prepared," she wrote in her final note, dated two years before her death, "I am very sorry. I should have told you. The basement must be maintained. The rituals are described in the red notebook in the locked desk in my study. Everything you need is there. The entity is dangerous only if the constraint lapses which it will do if unmaintained. I have kept it for sixty years. You may find it easier than you expect, or harder. It is simply a task. Like all tasks, it can be learned."

Callum had found the red notebook. He had found the locked desk. He had read everything.

He had then gone to the basement and stood at the door at the bottom of the stairs and looked at the room at the marks on the floor, at the specific arrangement of materials along the walls, at the quality of the air, which was cold and had a smell that was not mold, not damp, not any organic process, but something else, something without a natural analogue and he had felt the weight of sixty years of his grandmother's solitary management, and he had said out loud, to the room:

"I've read the notes."

Something changed in the quality of the silence.

"I understand what you are and what you want," he said. "And I understand that we're at the same impasse my grandmother was at. I'm not going to accept the original terms any more than she did."

The temperature dropped two degrees.

"But," he said, because he had been thinking about this for six weeks and he had a proposal that was his, not Heinrich Voss's, "I'm open to a different negotiation. Different terms. Not the terms my great-grandfather proposed."

The silence became alert. That was the only word he had for it. Attentive.

He spent the next four hours in the basement talking. He was a contract lawyer by profession. He had spent twenty years negotiating agreements between parties with opposing interests. He had never negotiated with anything like what was in the basement, but the fundamental logic identifying what each party actually needed versus what each party claimed to need, finding the overlap, building from there was the same.

By midnight he had the outline of something. Not a final agreement that took three further months, and it required resources he didn't have and had to acquire, and it required the entity to demonstrate a patience and a capacity for sustained negotiation that he would not have predicted.

But they reached an agreement. Not Heinrich's agreement. Something new.

Callum still does the maintenance. Some of it was in the original structure and he maintains it out of prudence. But the specific obligation of constraint is different now less confining, on both sides.

He has never told anyone the details of the agreement. He refers to it, when it comes up in conversation about his inheritance, as "a complicated estate situation."

This is not inaccurate.
