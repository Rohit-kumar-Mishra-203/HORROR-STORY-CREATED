Father Dominic had heard thirty years of confessions, and he believed had always believed that the worst things a human being could confess were the things they had done to other human beings. Violence. Betrayal. The long, slow cruelties that accumulated in families and marriages and workplaces and that people carried for decades before they could put them into words in the dark of the confessional box. These were the genuinely terrible things. Not the theological categories of sin but the actual human experience of guilt the weight of it, the way it deformed a life from the inside.

He had been wrong about this. He understood that now.

He understood it because of the man who came to confession on the third Tuesday of every month, for seven months, beginning in February of his twenty-ninth year of priesthood.

The first session was unremarkable. A man's voice middle-aged, educated, with a very slight accent that Father Dominic could not place. The confession was mundane: the man confessed to pride, to impatience with his family, to a habitual dishonesty in small social situations. Father Dominic gave a penance and absolution. The man thanked him and left.

The second session, in March, was different.

"Forgive me, Father, for I have sinned," the man said. The formula, ordinary. And then: "It has been one month since my last confession. In that time I have done the following things."

What followed was not a confession in any sense Father Dominic had encountered. It was a list precise, detailed, without remorse and without self-justification, in the flat tone of someone reading an inventory. The things listed were acts of harm: specific, directed, inflicted on people the man described as "available to me." The harm was not physical. It was more difficult to categorise than that. The man described with a specificity that made Father Dominic grip the screen between them with one hand the ways in which he had reached into the lives of certain individuals and systematically, methodically, destroyed something at their centre. A woman who had lost her ability to trust anyone. A man who had lost his ability to sleep without chemical assistance and didn't know why. A child Father Dominic's hand tightened a child who no longer spoke.

"These were not accidents," the man said. "I did these things intentionally. I caused this damage intentionally. I wish to confess it."

Father Dominic found his voice. "Do you seek absolution?" he asked, because the question was theological and he needed something theological to hold onto.

"I seek to confess," the man said. "The two are not always the same."

"They are in this sacrament."

"Perhaps," the man said, "this sacrament is being used in ways its designers did not anticipate."

Father Dominic sat with this for a moment. The confessional box was small and dark and close, and the warmth of it, which had always been a comfort the warm dark of privacy, of things said safely was not comforting now.

"You should leave," Father Dominic said.

"I know," the man said. "I will, in a moment. I want to tell you one more thing. I have been doing this confessing for a very long time. In many churches, in many cities, in many centuries. Not always Catholic churches. The specific theology doesn't matter. What matters is the act of telling someone. Of listing what I have done to a human being who must, by the rules of their practice, listen. There is something in the listening that I find " a pause " nourishing."

He left before Father Dominic could respond.

Father Dominic sat in the confessional for twenty minutes after he heard the outer door of the church close. He sat in the specific silence of a man who has understood something that changes the shape of everything he believed before.

The man came back in April.

Father Dominic had spent the intervening month consulting. He had called a bishop he trusted. He had read extensively in the theological literature on demonic presence, on the specific category of entities that interfered with the sacraments there was literature, more than he had expected, much of it very old and written in a register of absolute seriousness that the modern Church was ambivalent about but had not repudiated. He had also, in a departure from his usual resources, spoken to a professor of religious studies at the local university who had a specialty in comparative demonology and who looked at Father Dominic across a coffee shop table and said: "If what you're describing is accurate the specific pleasure it takes in the listening, the centuries of practice then you're dealing with something quite old. Something that has learned how to use the sacrament against itself."

"Can it be stopped?" Father Dominic had asked.

The professor had been quiet for a long time. "The older texts suggest that these entities can be made to speak their nature. If it confesses truly confesses, its actual nature rather than its actions the disclosure itself creates a vulnerability. But it must be its own words. It must choose to say what it is."

Father Dominic had not been sure he believed this. He had been even less sure that he knew how to bring it about. But April came and the man came back and Father Dominic was in the confessional, because being in the confessional was what he did.

The man began his list. Father Dominic listened as he always listened, with his whole attention, which was what this particular entity had always found nourishing. Halfway through the list, Father Dominic interrupted.

"Stop," he said.

"I want to hear something different," Father Dominic said. "Not what you have done. What you are."

Another silence longer. The quality of it was different from ordinary silence. It had weight.

"That is not what the sacrament asks for," the man said.

"No," Father Dominic agreed. "But I am asking. What are you? Not what you have done. What are you?"

A very long pause. Father Dominic felt the temperature in the confessional drop not dramatically, not cinematically, but perceptibly. A few degrees. The kind of cold that suggests the absence of something rather than the presence of something else.

"I am very old," the man said finally.

"I am " another pause, with a quality that Father Dominic could only think of as resistance "not what I appeared to be."

"No," Father Dominic said. "What are you?"

The voice changed. Not dramatically. But something in its pitch shifted downward, and something in its texture became less human in a way that Father Dominic could feel in his sternum.

"I am what remains," the voice said, "when everything that was human has been consumed. I have been consuming for I no longer count the years accurately. I use the sacred against the sacred because this is where the nourishment is densest. In the place where someone opens themselves where they make themselves available to be heard there is something exposed. Something I can access. I use your rituals because your rituals create access."

"And now?" Father Dominic said. "Now that you have said it?"

A very long silence.

"Now," the voice said, with something that was not quite anger and not quite surprise, "something is different."

The outer door of the church opened and closed. Father Dominic sat very still.

He never heard the voice again. The man never returned. Father Dominic retired from active ministry four years later, citing health reasons, and spent his final years writing extensively on what he called "the weaponisation of sacred space" a subject his bishop was profoundly uncomfortable with and which Father Dominic pursued with the complete determination of a man who has seen the enemy face a specific kind of defeat and wants to understand why it worked.

He died at eighty-one, in good faith, as he had always wished.

He left his papers to the university professor. They are not yet published. Some of them, the professor says, require further research before they can be responsibly released.

The professor says this with the face of someone who knows exactly what the papers say and is taking the required time to be ready.
