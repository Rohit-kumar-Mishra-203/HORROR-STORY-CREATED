THE GRIEF COUNSELLOR

Dr. Miriam Cole had been a grief counsellor for eighteen years. She had sat with people in the worst moments of their lives and she had learned through practice, through the accumulation of thousands of hours of close attention that grief was not a single thing. It was not the linear process that the popular models suggested. It was not a problem to be solved. It was a landscape, and every person moved through it differently, and the job of a grief counsellor was not to lead them through but to accompany them, to bear witness, to be a steady presence in the movement.

She believed this. She had built her practice on it. She was, by every measure, very good at her work.

She began seeing the client she thought of as "Mr. N." in autumn of the year she turned fifty. He came to her through a referral from a colleague who had retired abruptly and without explanation, which Miriam thought was odd but not concerning enough to pursue. Mr. N. presented as a man in his fifties, of indeterminate ethnicity, with the kind of face that was difficult to remember with precision she noticed this and attributed it, in her session notes, to his introversion, which sometimes gave a quality of slight elusiveness to the physical impression a person made.

He said he had lost his partner. He said his grief was complicated. He said he had been to other therapists and had not found them helpful, and that he had come to Miriam because he had heard she was particularly skilled with complicated cases.

She asked how his partner had died.

He paused for a long time. "They didn't die," he said finally. "They left. Not by choice. They were taken."

"Yes." Another long pause. "Not by anyone you could identify or apprehend. By something else."

Miriam made a note to explore this further the language of being "taken" was significant, suggested a specific relationship to the loss, possibly to agency and blame.

In subsequent sessions, she learned more. Mr. N. spoke about his partner with an intimacy and depth that suggested a relationship of extraordinary duration the detailed knowledge of another person that comes only from decades of close observation. He knew the specific sounds his partner made when thinking. He knew the particular way they moved in the early morning. He described these things with a quality of loving precision that Miriam found genuinely moving, and which she identified as a sign of profound attachment and therefore, unavoidably, of profound grief.

"How long were you together?" she asked, in the fourth session.

He looked at her in an odd way. "A very long time," he said.

"Can you be more specific?"

"No," he said. "Not really. It would seem implausible."

She moved on. She did not push in sessions when a client signalled a boundary; she circled back when the moment was right.

By the sixth session, something had begun to change that Miriam could not easily categorise. The change was in herself not in Mr. N., who remained consistent across sessions in a way she noted and considered clinically significant (grief, in her experience, was not consistent; it varied by the day, the hour, the specific memory triggered on a given morning). The change in herself was a growing exhaustion. Not the ordinary tiredness of emotional labour, which she was long accustomed to managing, but a specific depletion as if certain sessions left her not just tired but less, in some way she could not specify. As if something was being drawn off her in those sessions that wasn't being replenished in the ordinary ways.

She discussed this in supervision. Her supervisor, a woman in her sixties with forty years of clinical experience, listened carefully and said: "What do you give in these sessions? What is the quality of your presence with this client?"

Miriam thought about it. "Complete attention," she said. "Total openness. He needs I feel that he needs someone to be fully present. Not thinking, not processing just completely available."

Her supervisor was quiet for a moment. "That is a very specific therapeutic approach. For specific clients." She paused. "I would be careful. That level of openness that quality of making yourself entirely available is appropriate for certain situations. It can be, in other situations, an invitation you did not intend to issue."

Miriam thought about this. She thought about it for a week.

In the seventh session, she changed her approach. She maintained attention complete, focused attention but she did not open. She stayed behind what she thought of as a professional membrane the permeable boundary that allowed her to receive what a client offered without becoming the space through which something passed through her.

Mr. N. noticed immediately.

"You're different," he said. Not accusingly. Observationally.

"You've closed something," he said. "A door that was open before." He tilted his head a gesture that Miriam had seen before and that had always struck her as not quite right, the angle of it slightly off from what a human neck naturally does. "Why?"

"I'm trying a different approach," she said.

"The previous approach was more useful," he said.

He was quiet. "I come here to grieve," he said finally. "I have been grieving for a very long time. The grief is real I want you to understand that. What I lost was real and the loss is real. But I also " he paused " I require sustenance. And a person who is completely open, completely attending there is something in that quality of attention that is sustaining to me."

"Sustaining," Miriam said. "In what sense?"

"In the sense that I am very old and very tired and certain kinds of human contact replenish something that would otherwise be exhausted." He looked at her. "I have not harmed you. I have taken very little. I have tried to be responsible."

"What are you?" Miriam asked. The question came out before she decided to ask it her clinical instincts, perhaps, recognising that this was the moment.

The silence that followed was not the silence of hesitation. It was the silence of consideration.

"Something that was not always what it is now," he said. "Something very old, that was made different a very long time ago. The grief I described is real. The partner I lost was real. I lost them " his voice changed, lost some quality it had always had, became less managed " centuries ago. And I have been unable to stop grieving, and I have found that certain kinds of human presence ease the grief, and I have taken advantage of this, over the years, in ways that were perhaps not ethical."

"Not ethical," Miriam said.

"I never harmed anyone physically," he said. "But I used them without their knowledge. Without consent." He paused. "I am, I believe, aware of the problem with this."

"Why are you telling me?"

A very long pause. "Because you asked," he said. "And because you asked differently from the way people usually ask. As if you might actually receive the answer without running from it."

Miriam looked at him across the careful space of the therapy room. Outside, the city went about its business. Inside, she was sitting across from something that did not fit any category she had ever trained for.

"I can't continue treating you," she said.

"But " she paused, because what she was about to say was outside any clinical framework she had ever used " what you're describing. The grief. The inability to stop. There are there are approaches to that kind of grief. Not standard approaches. But things I've read about, in contexts I never expected to use."

"You would help me?" He sounded genuinely surprised.

"You came here for help," she said. "Whatever you are, that part seems real."

She helped him. It took six months of weekly sessions that she would never be able to write up or publish or discuss with colleagues. At the end of six months, something had shifted not disappeared, because she understood enough about grief to know it does not disappear but shifted into something more habitable. Less consuming.

He thanked her on his last visit with a specificity and sincerity that she found, unexpectedly, moving.

She increased her supervision hours for the following year. She found that she needed to talk about the work without being able to talk about the work, which is a specific kind of difficulty that therapists sometimes face.

Her supervisor, who was perceptive, asked once: "Was it worth it?"

Miriam thought about it. "I think so," she said. "I think something very old was carrying something very heavy. And it needed help carrying it."

"And the ethics?"

"Complicated," Miriam said. "As they always are."
