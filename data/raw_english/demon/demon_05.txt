THE LANGUAGE BENEATH LANGUAGE

Professor Anna Szymanska was a linguist who specialised in dead languages, and she had spent thirty years developing a reputation for finding things in ancient texts that other scholars had missed. She had published eleven books and something approaching four hundred papers. She was, by any measure, the foremost authority in her field on the linguistic patterns of pre-Roman European languages, and she was specifically celebrated for her ability to decode heavily damaged manuscripts where other specialists had found nothing.

This reputation was why the manuscript came to her.

It arrived via courier in a climate-controlled case, with documentation from a monastery in eastern Poland that had been, according to the accompanying note, "clearing archives as part of an ongoing renovation project." The note was from the monastery's librarian, a monk named Brother Tomasz who wrote in careful, formal academic English. He wrote that the manuscript had been found in a sealed section of the archive a section whose existence the current monks had not known about, accessed through a collapsed wall. He wrote that the manuscript predated the monastery by several centuries. He wrote that no one at the monastery could identify the script, and that the manuscript had an unusual effect on those who spent extended time with it, which he described, with what seemed like deliberate understatement, as "a tendency to produce unwelcome cognitive associations." He recommended no more than thirty minutes of continuous exposure, and suggested working in well-lit spaces with colleagues present.

Professor Szymanska had received eccentric conservation advice before. She filed Brother Tomasz's note and opened the case.

The manuscript was extraordinary. Thirty-seven pages of what appeared to be vellum, covered on both sides with a script of considerable elegance and obvious systematic organisation. The characters were unfamiliar but showed all the hallmarks of a developed writing system consistent symbol forms, clear directionality, evidence of ligatures and compound forms that suggested a language of some complexity. The ink was dark brown, possibly iron gall, possibly something else; she requested a chemical analysis. The vellum's dating, based on initial assessment, suggested somewhere in the range of the eighth to tenth century.

She began the work of analysis with great pleasure. The pleasure did not last.

The first anomaly she noticed was that the text resisted pattern recognition in a way that was inconsistent with any natural language. Natural languages all natural languages have regularities: frequencies of symbol occurrence, combinatorial rules, syntactic patterns. These regularities are how you begin to decode a text you don't know. You find the most frequent symbols and hypothesise that they are common letters or particles. You find the patterns of combination and hypothesise grammatical structure.

The manuscript's script had regularities, but they were wrong. They did not correspond to any pattern she could hypothesise a linguistic basis for. The frequency distributions were not like any natural language. The combinatorial patterns suggested rules, but rules of a kind she could not formulate in linguistic terms rules that seemed, the more she looked at them, to be doing something other than encoding speech.

"It's not a language," she said to her graduate student, Elena, who was helping with the analysis. "Not in any sense I can apply. It's it's not encoding meaning the way language encodes meaning."

"Then what is it encoding?" Elena asked.

"I don't know," Szymanska said. And then: "That's not quite right. I have a hypothesis. I don't want to state it because it's not a linguistic hypothesis."

She stated it to herself, in private: the text was not encoding meaning, it was encoding effect. Not what it said but what it did. Not communication but action. A set of instructions, perhaps, but instructions addressed not to a reader but to something else something that would respond not to comprehension but to the act of the symbols being present and correctly arranged.

She worked on it for three months. She became, as the weeks passed, increasingly reluctant to work on it alone. She began bringing Elena to all sessions. She kept the lights very bright. She worked in the morning, when she was most herself, and stopped by noon, because she found that afternoon work produced a specific quality of mental tiredness that she disliked.

She found, in the twelfth week, a section she thought of as "different" a passage of approximately two pages in which the character forms changed subtly. Not drastically; a scholar who was not looking carefully might have missed it. But Szymanska was always looking carefully, and she saw it: the same system of symbols but arranged differently not as a text to be read but as something that could, she thought, be spoken. As something that had been written down from an oral original.

She almost tried to read it aloud.

She stopped herself before she did, with the sudden, cold clarity of someone who has been walking in fog and has suddenly seen the edge of the cliff she's approaching.

She did not know why she stopped. She had no rational basis for the conviction that reading this passage aloud would be different. Dangerous. But the conviction was absolute and she trusted it with a rapidity that her rational mind could not have produced. Some older part of her mind, some deeper faculty, produced the conviction fully formed and completely compelling.

She closed the manuscript.

She wrote to Brother Tomasz. She described her findings in technical terms and then, after a pause, dropped the technical terms and wrote plainly: "I believe this document was created with a specific purpose that I cannot describe in linguistic terms, and that engaging with it further poses risks I cannot fully characterise. I am returning it. I would recommend that access to it be permanently restricted. I understand this conflicts with the principle of scholarly accessibility. I can only say that I have been working with manuscripts for thirty years and I have never before recommended that one be locked away. I am recommending it now."

Brother Tomasz responded: "We understand. We have been expecting its return. It has been placed in the sealed archive from which it came, and the wall has been repaired. The archive will remain closed. Thank you for your work, and for your prudence."

Szymanska published two papers from her three months of analysis careful, technical papers about the structural anomalies of the script, framed as a linguistic puzzle with no proposed solution. They were well received. Several colleagues expressed interest in seeing the manuscript.

She told them it had been returned to its source and was no longer available for study. She told them this with a firmness that discouraged follow-up questions.

She did not tell them about the passage that could be spoken aloud. She did not describe the feeling of almost reading it. She did not explain why, after thirty years of working in well-lit offices alone, she now always works with a colleague present and always in the morning and always with the door open.

Some discoveries, she had concluded, were discoveries in the sense of "we have found something." They were not discoveries in the sense of "we should engage with this further." The distinction was not one that the academy was comfortable with.

She was comfortable with it. She was very comfortable with it.
