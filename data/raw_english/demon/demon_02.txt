THE BARGAIN AT THE CROSSROADS

The crossroads where the old highway crossed the even older county road was marked on no map as anything other than an intersection, but it had been a place of particular significance for several hundred years, and the locals those who were old enough to know, which was not many anymore called it "the meeting point" with a specificity that had nothing to do with directions.

Josie Hale came to the crossroads at midnight on the first Sunday of November, because she was twenty-three years old and her little brother was dying and she had run out of rational options and had moved, in her desperation, to irrational ones.

Her brother, Tommy, was nineteen. He had been diagnosed eight months ago with a condition that the doctors described in terms that Josie could understand individually but that lost her at the level of what they meant together. She had understood: serious. Progressive. No treatment with reliable outcomes. She had understood: we are doing everything we can. She had understood: you should prepare. He had deteriorated quickly. He was in hospital now, in a room where machines tracked his persistence in the world and where their parents sat in shifts and where Josie went every day and held his hand and talked to him about things they had done as children, as if the talking could anchor him to a life he had not finished living yet.

She had read about crossroads. She was not stupid she knew the folklore, knew the specific folklore of bargains made at the intersection of roads, of the specific entity that was said to attend such places at midnight. She knew that the folklore was fictional, was metaphorical, was a way of encoding human anxieties about desperation and desire and the dangerous shortcuts people take when they are out of conventional options.

She came anyway. Because Tommy was nineteen and the machines said two weeks, maybe three, and she had nothing left.

The crossroads at midnight in November was cold and dark and utterly silent. There was no traffic this road no longer carried any meaningful volume of vehicles. She stood at the precise centre of the intersection, where the painted lines crossed, and she stood there for twenty minutes feeling profoundly alone and vaguely stupid and then she heard footsteps.

They came from she could not determine a direction. They seemed to come from the crossroads itself, from somewhere beneath the asphalt, from the specific geometry of intersecting lines. The footsteps were unhurried, and the figure they belonged to resolved gradually out of the dark at the edge of her vision not from one direction, but from everywhere at once, as if the dark had collected itself and made something.

The figure was ordinary was the word that surprised her. Not dramatically inhuman. Not the horned or smoking or dramatically terrifying figure of central folklore. A person of indeterminate age and unremarkable appearance, dressed in dark, practical clothing, who stood at the edge of the crossroads and looked at her with an attention that was total and that told her, more clearly than any physical anomaly, that this was not an ordinary person.

"You know what this place is," the figure said. Statement, not question.

"I've read about it," Josie said.

"And you came anyway."

"My brother is dying," she said.

"I know," the figure said. "I am aware of everyone in your immediate situation."

"Can you help him?"

A pause. The pause was not theatrical not a dramatic hesitation for effect. It was the pause of something considering something with genuine care.

"I can," the figure said. "This is within my capacity. But I want to discuss with you what that means before we proceed, because the versions of this conversation that folklore records are incomplete in ways that have caused a great deal of unnecessary suffering."

Josie blinked. "What?"

"The traditional narrative," the figure said, "involves a bargain in which the supplicant gains something and loses something talent, years of life, their soul, various currencies of human value. This narrative is not entirely inaccurate, but it is presented as inevitable and as without alternative, which is not the case." The figure paused. "I am very old and I have conducted many of these conversations and the one thing I find genuinely repugnant is the transactional model in which suffering is treated as a commodity. Your brother's life is not a transaction. Your desperation is not a lever."

"Then " Josie was not sure how to proceed. "Then what are you offering?"

"I am not offering," the figure said. "I am asking. What do you actually need? Not what the folklore says you should ask for. Not a vague wish. What does your brother need, specifically, and what is the actual obstacle?"

They talked for an hour and a half. Not in the way folklore described not in the language of bargains and prices and supernatural exchanges. In the language of specifics: what was the diagnosis, what were the treatment options that had been tried, what had not been tried and why. The figure asked precise questions and listened to the answers with the focused attention of someone who had access to a very large body of information and was navigating it in real time.

At the end of the hour and a half, the figure said: "There is a treatment. Not a supernatural intervention a medical one, developed four years ago, currently in a clinical trial in a research hospital in the Netherlands. Your brother's specific presentation makes him an unusual candidate, but a candidate. The trial has space. The lead researcher is accessible." The figure paused. "I can ensure the referral reaches the right desk by Monday morning, if you will do something for me in return."

"Tell people about this conversation," the figure said. "Not the dramatic version. The accurate version. The version in which someone at the end of their options came to a crossroads and was met with not a mystical bargain, but practical help from a direction they hadn't expected. The version in which desperation does not have to mean exploitation."

Josie stared at it. "That's what you want? A story?"

"Stories change how people approach their desperation," the figure said. "If one more person, in the future, comes to a crossroads expecting exploitation and finds instead that there are other possibilities yes. That is what I want."

The referral arrived on Monday morning. The trial coordinator called by Wednesday. Tommy was transferred to the Netherlands in December.

He is twenty-five now. He still has limitations from the illness. He is alive. He plays guitar badly and is studying to be an environmental scientist and argues with Josie about politics every Sunday call.

Josie has told the story several times. Not the dramatic version. The accurate version. Most people don't quite know what to do with it.

She tells it anyway.
