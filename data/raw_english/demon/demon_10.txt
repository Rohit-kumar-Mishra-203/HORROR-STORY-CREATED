THE TENANT BELOW

Nobody moved into Apartment B.

This was the fact that nobody in the building could quite explain. A had the Parkers, a middle-aged couple who kept odd hours and a small, silent dog. C had been occupied for two years by a young woman named Tara who played piano badly and enthusiastically every Sunday morning. The building had twelve apartments across four floors, and eleven of them were occupied. B was not.

The landlord, Gerald Moss, said the apartment was under renovation. He said this consistently and without elaboration for two years. Nobody had ever seen workers going in or out. Nobody had ever heard drilling or hammering. But the light under the door of B changed this was something several residents had noticed and not discussed, the way you don't discuss things that make you feel you might be going slightly insane. Sometimes the light was off. Sometimes it was on, a warm amber glow. Sometimes, very rarely, it was something else a deep red that bled under the door like spilled wine and that the residents, when they happened to pass at the wrong moment, would instinctively step away from and then never think about again with any clarity.

Marcus Webb moved into B, directly above, in October. He was thirty-one, recently divorced, a software developer who worked from home and who had chosen this building specifically because it was quiet and close to nothing in particular. He wanted quiet. He wanted anonymity. He wanted a place that didn't know his history.

He got all of these things, and one more thing he had not asked for.

He first noticed the sounds on his second night. They came from below from B, which was directly beneath him. Not loud sounds: that was the disturbing thing. Loud sounds from below would have been ordinary, would have been neighbours, would have been the reassuring noise of other people living their lives. These sounds were quiet. Deliberate in their quietness, as if whatever was making them was aware of being heard and was making a specific choice about volume. A low, rhythmic scraping. A sound like something being dragged across stone, though the floors were hardwood. And underneath all of it so low that Marcus felt it in his chest rather than heard it with his ears a vibration that had no obvious source and no obvious name.

He went to Gerald Moss the next morning. "The apartment below me," he said. "Is someone in there?"

Moss looked at him steadily. "Under renovation," he said.

"I heard sounds last night."

"Contractors sometimes work odd hours."

"At three in the morning?"

Moss said nothing. His eyes moved away from Marcus's face to a point slightly past his left shoulder, and they stayed there until Marcus left.

Marcus began keeping a log. He was a methodical person it was what made him good at his job and methodology felt like the correct response to irrationality. He wrote down dates, times, durations, descriptions of sounds. October th: scraping sound, : AM, duration approximately twenty minutes. October th: vibration in floor, no audible sound, : AM, I could feel it in my feet through the mattress. October th: something that sounded like a voice, deeply pitched, no words distinguishable, : AM exactly.

October nd: I saw something in the mirror.

He had a full-length mirror on the back of his bedroom door. He'd placed it there without thought it was a convenient location. He was passing it at roughly midnight, going to the kitchen for water, when he saw in its reflection not the door behind him but a room he did not recognise. A stone room, lit by a low reddish light, with walls that curved inward and upward in a way that suggested great depth below rather than height above. He saw it for perhaps three seconds. Then the mirror showed his bedroom door, and his own pale face looking at the mirror from six inches away.

He turned the mirror to face the wall.

November arrived and the sounds changed. They became purposeful was the word Marcus kept returning to, and it was a word that frightened him because it implied intention, and intention implied a mind. The scraping had been random. These new sounds were not random. They occurred at the same time each night : AM, always : AM and they seemed to be coming from directly below his bed. Not below his floor; below his specific position in the room. As if something had determined where he slept and placed itself precisely there.

On November th, he found the marks on the floor.

He'd moved the bed to look some impulse he couldn't fully account for. Beneath the bed, on the hardwood, were marks he had not made: a rough circle, about two feet in diameter, scratched into the wood from below. Not scratched pressed. The wood was deformed upward, very slightly, as if tremendous pressure had been applied from beneath. Within the circle were lines that were not random; they had a pattern, a geometry that pulled the eye in an uncomfortable direction, that created the visual sensation of depth where depth was impossible.

He photographed the marks. He sent the photographs to three different people a friend, his sister, a former colleague who he remembered mentioning an interest in occult symbology as a strange hobby. His friend said: weird, what is that, some kind of art installation? His sister said: you should call the landlord. The former colleague Daniel Park called him back within an hour and asked, without preamble: "Where are you living?"

"Because those marks," Daniel said, "are a summoning boundary. Not a complete one whoever made them didn't finish. But they're the opening section of a binding sequence I've only seen in one context before, in a book that is very old and very specific about what these sequences are for." A pause. "Marcus, what's below your apartment?"

Marcus told him about B. About the light under the door. About two years of "renovation."

Daniel drove four hours to see him that weekend.

He was not what Marcus had expected from someone who studied occult symbology he was a compact, precise man in his late forties who wore clean practical clothes and who examined the marks under Marcus's bed with the systematic attention of an engineer looking at a structural fault.

"Someone has been maintaining a presence," Daniel said finally. "Long term. The marks on your floor aren't new they're the result of whatever is in B pressing against the boundary of its containment over a long period. Like water damage, but " he gestured at the marks " less water, more something else."

"What is in B?" Marcus asked.

Daniel sat back on his heels and looked up at him. "Something that was put there intentionally," he said. "Something that is contained. Imperfectly contained, and the containment is eroding which is what you've been hearing. These marks are the sound made visible, if you want to think of it that way. The thing below is pushing up against the edges of what holds it."

"What holds it?"

"The apartment itself, partly. Gerald Moss knows he's maintained it for two years, which means he's been managing the containment. Not perfectly, but enough." Daniel paused. "The question is what happens when it stops being enough."

"And what happens?"

Daniel looked at the marks for a long moment. "The boundary breaks," he said quietly. "And whatever is down there is no longer in B."

Marcus moved out the following week. He was not a coward he told himself this repeatedly but he was rational, and rationality had a clear recommendation here.

He learned, six months later, that Gerald Moss had died in November. A heart attack, officially. Marcus thought about the timing. He thought about Daniel's phrase: managing the containment.

He thought about who managed it now.

He didn't call the building. He didn't go back. Some doors, once seen from the right angle, should not be opened further.

But on certain nights the nights when the city is very quiet and his new apartment's floors are very still he lies awake and thinks about : AM. He thinks about the circle pressed upward into wood from below. He thinks about all the people still sleeping in that building above whatever Gerald Moss had spent two years containing.

He keeps his mirrors face-to-the-wall now.

All of them.
