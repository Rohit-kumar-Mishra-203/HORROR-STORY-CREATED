Story : The Glass-Witch of Stourbridge
The sky above Stourbridge in the s was rarely blue. It was a bruised, industrial grey, painted by the relentless belching of coal smoke from the towering, brick-built glass cones that dominated the skyline. Inside these massive, kiln-like structures, the heat was a physical, oppressive force, routinely exceeding C. It was a brutal environment that demanded sweat, precision, and an iron will.

Eliza possessed all three. She was a master glassblower, a rarity for a woman in a trade dominated by soot-stained, heavily muscled men. But Eliza had an advantage that the men of the Red House Cone did not: she did not merely shape the glass; she listened to it.

Eliza was a glass-witch.

Her magic was intimately tied to the breath. When a glassblower gathers molten silica on the end of a hollow steel pipe and blows, they are sharing their own life force their literal breath with the inanimate material, expanding it, giving it form and purpose. Eliza discovered that if she held a specific memory or a potent emotion in her mind at the exact moment she exhaled into the pipe, the glass would capture it.

It started as a beautiful, melancholic accident. Grieving the recent loss of her mother, Eliza had been blowing a simple paperweight. As she breathed, a single, sharp memory of her mother laughing in a sunlit garden filled her mind. When the glass cooled, it was not clear. Suspended within the solid, heavy sphere was a swirling, luminous ribbon of pure, golden light. When Eliza touched the cool glass, she didn't just remember the laugh; she felt the exact, profound warmth of that summer day wash over her.

She had successfully trapped an emotion.

For a time, her secret was a quiet cottage industry. She worked the night shifts, taking the leftover 'gather' from the crucibles to craft her specialized baubles. She sold them discreetly to those desperate for a reprieve from the grim Victorian reality. To a grieving widow, she sold a tear-drop pendant filled with the calming blue mist of acceptance. To a terrified young soldier heading off to foreign wars, a heavy marble containing the fierce, red spark of raw courage.

But secrets in Stourbridge, much like the glass itself, were fragile things. Whispers of the "Memory-Maker" eventually drifted out of the soot-choked alleys and reached the opulent, manicured estates of the aristocracy.

They reached the decaying ears of Lord Silas Blackwood.

Lord Blackwood was a man rotting from the inside out. Decades of unchecked indulgence and a mysterious, wasting disease had left him a skeletal, trembling husk of a man, barely clinging to his early sixties. He possessed unimaginable wealth, but all the gold in the British Empire could not bribe the Reaper.

When Blackwood s velvet-lined carriage arrived at Eliza s modest workshop on cobbled High Street, it felt like a hearse pulling up to a tomb.

Two massive footmen forced their way into the shop, dragging a terrified boy between them. It was Thomas, Eliza s twelve-year-old apprentice. Blackwood followed, leaning heavily on a silver-tipped cane, his breathing a wet, rattling wheeze.

"They tell me you bottle feelings, girl," Blackwood rasped, his eyes the color of dirty ice. "They say you can trap the essence of a human soul in your pretty little trinkets."

Eliza stood her ground, gripping a heavy iron pontil rod tightly. "I trap memories, My Lord. Fleeting things. It is parlor magic, nothing more. Let the boy go."

"I have no interest in parlor magic," Blackwood sneered, coughing into a silk handkerchief that came away stained with blood. "I am dying. My body is failing, but my mind is razor-sharp. I refuse to let it extinguish. If you can trap a memory, you can trap a consciousness. You will forge me a vessel. You will draw the life out of this useless urchin to prime it, and then you will transfer me into a flawless, eternal crystal."

Eliza felt the blood drain from her face. "You speak of soul-theft. That is dark, forbidden magic. The glass cannot hold a human soul; the pressure would shatter it, and it would kill the boy instantly."

"Then you had better blow the glass very thick, my dear," Blackwood whispered, a cruel smile stretching his papery skin. "Because if you refuse, my men will throw your little apprentice into the main crucible at the Red House Cone. I imagine he will melt quite quickly."

He gave her no choice. The footmen dragged Thomas away, locked in a carriage bound for Blackwood Manor, and Eliza was forced to follow.

Deep in the subterranean cellars of Blackwood s estate, the mad lord had constructed a profane imitation of a Stourbridge glasshouse. A massive furnace roared in the center of the stone dungeon, fueled not by coal, but by strange, pungent woods that burned with a sickly violet flame. Thomas was bound to a wooden chair near the unbearable heat, weeping silently.

"Begin," Blackwood commanded, collapsing into a velvet armchair, his chest heaving with the effort of simply existing.

Eliza approached the crucible. She took a fresh blowpipe, dipping it into the blinding heart of the violet fire. She turned the pipe, gathering a massive, glowing orb of molten glass. It was heavier than anything she had ever worked with, thick and sluggish.

She moved to the marver a polished iron table and began to roll the glowing gather, shaping it into a cylinder. The heat blistering her face was intense, but it was nothing compared to the cold terror in her heart.

Think, she commanded herself. She understood the thermodynamics of glass, and she understood the arcane mechanics of her magic. Blackwood wanted a vacuum. He wanted her to pull Thomas s youthful essence out and trap it, creating an energetic void that his own decaying soul could rush into.

But glass was a two-way medium. It required an exhale to shape it, but the pressure could be reversed.

Eliza brought the pipe to her lips. She looked at Thomas, projecting absolute calm, and then she looked at Lord Blackwood, who was leaning forward, his eyes wide with desperate, greedy anticipation.

She did not draw upon Thomas. Instead, Eliza breathed her own profound, righteous fury into the pipe.

She pushed her magic into the molten silica, shaping it not into a simple bauble, but into a complex, multi-layered labyrinth of glass. She blew a sphere within a sphere, creating a trap of mirrored, reflective surfaces on the inside.

As the glass expanded, glowing a brilliant, blinding white, Eliza began to chant in the old tongue of the hearth-witches.

"What are you doing?" Blackwood demanded, struggling to stand, his cane clattering to the stone floor. "Draw the boy! Draw his life!"

Eliza ignored him. With a final, agonizing push of her breath, she finalized the internal structure. Then, she stepped off the wooden bench and strode directly toward the aristocratic lord.

"You wanted immortality, Lord Blackwood," Eliza shouted over the roar of the furnace. "You wanted to be preserved in perfect, flawless crystal!"

She raised the blowpipe. The massive, glowing orb of glass hovered inches from Blackwood s terrified face.

Eliza sharply inhaled, reversing the flow of her magic.

She didn't pull breath; she pulled essence. She created an arcane vacuum within the complex glass labyrinth.

Blackwood screamed, but no sound came out. A thick, oily black vapor erupted from his eyes, his nose, and his screaming mouth. It was the distilled essence of his cruelty, his greed, and his desperately clinging consciousness. The vacuum inside the glass demanded to be filled, and it tore Blackwood s soul from his decaying body with the force of a hurricane.

The black vapor whipped through the air and was sucked violently into the blowpipe, shooting down the steel barrel and slamming into the molten glass.

Eliza instantly capped the mouthpiece with her thumb, sealing the vacuum. She swung the pipe down and struck the iron pontil rod with a sharp, practiced blow. The glass bauble snapped off the pipe, perfectly sealed.

It hit the stone floor but did not shatter.

Silence fell over the cellar, broken only by the crackle of the violet flames.

In the velvet chair, Lord Blackwood s body slumped backward. His eyes were open and glassy, staring blankly at the ceiling. He was still breathing a shallow, mechanical rasp but there was nobody home. He was an empty, brain-dead husk.

On the floor, the glass bauble rapidly cooled. It lost its glowing heat, turning into a heavy, perfectly smooth sphere of lead crystal. But inside, trapped within the intricate, mirrored layers Eliza had blown, a furious storm of black, oily smoke swirled endlessly. It battered against the impenetrable walls of the glass, a trapped, immortal consciousness screaming in a prison of its own making.

Eliza dropped the blowpipe. She was exhausted, her hands trembling and her lungs burning, but she moved quickly. She grabbed a pair of iron shears and cut the ropes binding Thomas to the chair.

"Come on," she gasped, pulling the boy toward the cellar stairs. "We have to go."

"Is he... is he dead?" Thomas whispered, looking in horror at the slumped aristocrat.

"No," Eliza said coldly, glancing down at the heavy glass bauble resting on the stones. "He got exactly what he paid for. He s going to live forever."

They fled Blackwood Manor, slipping out into the freezing, smog-choked Stourbridge night. Eliza knew they could never return to her workshop on High Street. The authorities would find the empty shell of Lord Blackwood, and while they might not understand the magic, they would recognize the murder.

But as they disappeared into the labyrinth of brick and canal water, Eliza felt a strange sense of peace. She had lost her home, but she had protected her craft. And somewhere in the dark cellar of a ruined estate, a cruel man was finally learning the true weight of eternity, trapped forever in a masterpiece of Victorian glass.
