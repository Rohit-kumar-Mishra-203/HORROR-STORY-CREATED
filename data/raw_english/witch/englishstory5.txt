Story : The Bog-Mother of the Fens
The Fens of Blackwood were not beautiful in the traditional sense. There were no sweeping vistas, no majestic mountain peaks, and no sparkling, crystal-clear streams. Instead, it was a landscape of horizontal lines and muted colors: a vast, flat expanse of brown peat, grey mist, and the dark, still mirrors of standing water. It smelled of decay, wet sphagnum moss, and time immemorial.

To the people of the nearby towns, the Fens were a wasteland, a place where sheep wandered off to drown and where the dense fog played cruel tricks on the eyes. But to Morag, the Fens were a fortress.

Morag was the Bog-Mother. If you asked the local children, they would tell you she had teeth like rusty nails and ate disobedient toddlers. The reality was slightly less dramatic, though no less intimidating. Morag was old older than the crumbling stone bridge at the edge of the county, older than the records in the parish church. She wore cloaks woven from dried reeds and her skin had taken on the tough, leathery texture of the bog bodies she guarded.

Her magic was the magic of preservation. The peat bogs were anaerobic, devoid of oxygen, capable of keeping things perfectly suspended in time. Morag used this natural power to maintain a massive, invisible seal. Centuries ago, a sickness of the earth known as the Ash-Rot a sentient blight that turned soil to dust and choked the life from the roots of the world had been cornered here. The ancient covens had trapped it deep beneath the peat, relying on the crushing weight of the waterlogged earth to keep it dormant. Morag was the last of her line, the sole warden of a prison nobody remembered existed.

For centuries, her watch had been quiet. But progress is a relentless, unfeeling thing.

The trouble began with a low, mechanical thrum that vibrated through the water table, giving Morag a splitting headache. The Sterling Land Corporation had purchased the "useless" marshland for pennies. Their grand vision involved a network of deep drainage canals, monstrous diesel-powered pumps, and acres of newly reclaimed, highly profitable farmland.

They didn't know they were pulling the cork from a bottle of poison.

Morag was standing knee-deep in a patch of cattails, trying to mend a fraying magical ward, when she heard the splash. It wasn't the heavy, clumsy flounder of a stray sheep. It was the frantic, thrashing sound of a human.

She waded silently through the reeds, the mud parting for her like an old friend. In a shallow pool near the edge of the deeper bogs, a boy was flailing. He couldn't have been older than fourteen, wearing a rain-soaked oversized jacket and carrying a canvas backpack that was actively dragging him under.

Morag sighed, a sound like wind rustling dead leaves. She reached out, her gnarled hand grasping the collar of his jacket, and hauled him out of the muck with deceptive, wiry strength. She dumped him unceremoniously onto a patch of solid heather.

The boy coughed up muddy water and scrambled backward, his eyes wide with terror as he took in Morag s mud-streaked face and reed-woven hair.

"P-please," he stammered, scrambling for a pocketknife that was hopelessly jammed in his wet jeans. "I don't have any money."

"If I wanted money, boy, I wouldn't live in a swamp," Morag grunted, turning her back on him to inspect the nearby water. "What are you doing in the Fens? The drowning pools don't need feeding today."

"I'm hiding," the boy said, shivering violently as the autumn wind cut through his wet clothes. "From the police. And my foster dad. I'm Toby."

Morag glanced at him over her shoulder. She saw the bruises on his wrists, the defensive hunch of his shoulders, and the desperate, cornered-animal look in his eyes. The Fens recognized outcasts. It was a haven for things that had nowhere else to go.

"Well, Toby," she said, her voice softening just a fraction. "You picked a terrible week to hide here. The world is coming to tear the roof off my house, and they re going to kill everyone in the county doing it."

Before Toby could ask what she meant, the earth shuddered violently. A mechanical roar, infinitely louder than before, tore through the quiet mist. Half a mile away, the towering, yellow arm of a massive industrial excavator rose above the tree line. It plunged its steel bucket deep into the earth.

Morag felt the impact in her bones. A sharp, burning sensation sliced across her chest. The ward had cracked.

"No, no, no," she hissed, abandoning her slow, measured pace. She began to sprint across the bog, her bare feet barely touching the sinking mud.

Toby, driven by a sudden, inexplicable fear of being left alone in the mist, scrambled up and ran after her. "Wait! What's happening?"

"They are draining the deep trench!" Morag yelled back over the roar of the engines. "They are letting the air in!"

When they reached the edge of the construction site, the sight was horrifying. Five massive excavators were tearing massive gouges into the peat. Huge diesel pumps were already chugging, sucking the ancient water out of the bog and spewing it into a nearby river.

But it wasn't just water coming up.

From the deepest freshly dug trench, a sickly, pale grey smoke was beginning to seep. Where the smoke touched the heather, the plants instantly withered, crumbling into fine white ash. The air tasted of sulfur and dry rot. The Ash-Rot was waking up.

The construction workers in their bright orange vests hadn't noticed yet, deafened by their machines and blinded by their progress.

"I have to stop the pumps," Morag said, her voice tight with panic. "But I need to seal the earth simultaneously, or the pressure change will blow the Rot directly into the atmosphere. I cannot be in two places at once."

She looked down at Toby. He was trembling, staring at the grey smoke that was slowly creeping toward a line of birch trees, turning their bark to dust.

"Boy," Morag said, grabbing his shoulders. "Toby. Do you want to prove you're braver than the people you ran from?"

Toby swallowed hard, looking from the terrifying witch to the encroaching grey death, and nodded.

"Take this," she commanded, pressing a heavy, smooth stone carved with spiraling runes into his hands. It pulsed with a cool, damp energy. "Run to the edge of that trench. When I stop the machines, the smoke will surge. You must throw this stone directly into the deepest part of the water and yell the word 'Bind'. Do not hesitate. Do not breathe the smoke."

Without waiting for his confirmation, Morag turned to face the machines. She didn't use subtle magic this time. She drew upon the raw, crushing weight of the Fens. She raised her arms, her fingers hooked like talons.

The bog responded. The water levels in the surrounding pools dropped instantly as Morag pulled the moisture into the air. With a deafening crack, the soft, muddy ground beneath the massive, multi-ton excavators simply liquefied.

The heavy machines groaned as they sank rapidly into the mire, their tracks churning uselessly in the sudden quicksand. The workers yelled in panic, abandoning their controls and scrambling for solid ground.

Morag turned her attention to the massive diesel pumps. She threw her hands forward. A surge of thick, sludgy bog-water shot out of the canals like a geyser, slamming directly into the intake valves of the pumps. The engines sputtered, choked on the mud, and died with a series of loud, metallic clanks.

The sudden silence was deafening, broken only by the panicked shouts of the fleeing workers.

But as the pumps stopped, the pressure equilibrium broke. The grey smoke in the trench violently erupted, bursting upward in a thick, choking cloud of pure rot. It surged over the lip of the trench, rushing toward Morag.

"Now, Toby!" she screamed, dropping to her knees as the magic exhausted her.

Toby had crept to the edge of the trench. The smell of the smoke was agonizing, making his eyes water and his throat burn. He saw the grey wave rushing out, turning the mud to dry dust in seconds.

He didn't run. He leaned over the precipice, aiming for the small pool of black water still remaining at the bottom. He threw the runestone with all his might.

"BIND!" his adolescent voice cracked, ringing out clear over the quiet bog.

The stone hit the water.

Instantly, the magic of the Fens reacted. The stagnant water in the trench rushed upward, forming a solid, crystalline wall of dark ice that slammed over the opening of the earth like a heavy vault door. The grey smoke hit the barrier and shrieked a high, unnatural sound before being sucked back down into the dark, starved of the oxygen it needed to survive.

The Fens fell completely still. The excavators were half-buried in the mud, ruined beyond repair. The workers were long gone, fleeing back to the safety of the paved roads.

Morag sat heavily in the mud, panting, looking older and more tired than she had an hour ago. Toby carefully picked his way back to her, his clothes still soaked, his hands shaking, but his chin held high.

"Is it... is it over?" he asked quietly.

Morag looked at the ruined machinery, then down at the boy who hadn't run away. The developers would be back, eventually. They would bring lawyers and more machines. But the bog had proven it wouldn't go quietly, and perhaps, for the first time in centuries, Morag realized she didn't have to fight them alone.

"For today, yes," the Bog-Mother rasped, a tiny, rare smile cracking the mud on her face. "You did well, Toby. Now, come along. Let's get you back to my hearth. You need a cup of nettle tea, and I need to teach you how to weave a proper warding spell. If you're going to live in my swamp, you're going to earn your keep."

Toby didn't argue. He adjusted his heavy backpack and followed the witch deeper into the mist, the sound of their footsteps disappearing into the quiet, ancient rhythm of the Fens.
