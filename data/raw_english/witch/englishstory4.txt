Story : The Ink-Stained Witch of Oxford
To the casual observer, Beatrice Vance was merely another fixture of the Duke Humfrey s Library, as permanent and unassuming as the oak reading desks or the scent of decaying lignin and beeswax that permeated the air. She wore thick, tortoiseshell glasses, kept her hair in a severe bun that defied gravity, and possessed an encyclopedic knowledge of the Dewey Decimal System that bordered on the supernatural.

But Beatrice s true supernatural talents had nothing to do with cataloging. She was an ink-witch.

Her gift had manifested slowly. It began when she was a child, reading Alice s Adventures in Wonderland. She had traced her finger over a John Tenniel illustration of the White Rabbit, and for a fleeting, terrifying second, she felt the unmistakable tickle of soft fur against her fingertip. By the time she was a postgraduate student at Oxford, she had honed the ability. With enough concentration, Beatrice could coax the ink off a page, weaving the two-dimensional text and illustrations into three-dimensional, semi-solid constructs.

She kept her magic strictly contained. It was a private comfort. On rainy Tuesday mornings, she might summon a tiny, fluttering sparrow from a volume of British ornithology to keep her company while she sorted archives. Or, if she was feeling particularly exhausted, she could pull a phantom cup of steaming Earl Grey from a Dickens novel it tasted faintly of parchment and didn't actually hydrate her, but the warmth was real enough.

Her quiet, carefully ordered world unraveled on the morning of the Autumn Equinox.

Beatrice arrived at the library before dawn, as was her custom, to check the environmental controls in the subterranean Restricted Archival Vault. This vault housed texts that were not merely rare, but dangerous. Books bound in questionable leathers, scrolls that whispered when the temperature dropped, and grimoires that pulsed with dark, trapped energy.

When Beatrice reached the bottom of the spiraling stone staircase, her breath hitched. The heavy iron door to the vault, secured by three separate mechanical locks and a warding spell placed by the Head Librarian in , hung wide open. The metal around the hinges was warped and partially melted, dripping like hot wax.

Panic, cold and sharp, pierced her chest. She sprinted into the vault. The air inside tasted of ozone, copper, and burnt sugar the unmistakable residue of forceful, chaotic magic. She scanned the rows of specialized glass cases.

Only one was broken. The glass was shattered outward, glittering on the stone floor like frost. The velvet cushion inside was empty.

The Codex Noctis was gone.

The Codex was not a book of fiction, nor was it a traditional spellbook. It was a bestiary compiled by a mad th-century monk who had attempted to catalog the nightmares of the human subconscious. The creatures depicted within its pages were horrors of anatomy and malice. If brought into the waking world, they wouldn't just cause physical damage; they would feed on the sanity of anyone who saw them.

Beatrice knelt by the shattered glass. She closed her eyes and extended her senses, feeling for the lingering magical vibration. The thief hadn't just taken the book; they had opened it. A trail of invisible, heavy magical pressure led out of the vault, up the stairs, and out into the slumbering city of Oxford.

She didn't have time to call the Constabulary of Arcane Regulation. By the time they filed the necessary paperwork to deploy an agent, the thief would have figured out how to fully manifest the horrors within the Codex. Beatrice grabbed her heavy canvas messenger bag, hastily shoved three specific books from her own desk into it, and ran out into the chilly morning fog.

The trail of dark magic was easy for an ink-witch to follow. It left a stain on the world, a subtle warping of the shadows and a foul stench that made the gargoyles on the university buildings seem to weep black soot. The trail led her away from the central campus, winding down narrow, cobblestone alleys toward an abandoned Victorian lecture hall near the edge of the river.

The heavy wooden doors of the hall were barred from the inside, but Beatrice could hear a voice chanting in a rhythmic, frantic cadence. She pressed her hand against the wood, visualizing the internal iron latch. She whispered a passage from a structural engineering textbook she had memorized, commanding the ink-memory of the metal to shift. With a dull clack, the latch slid open.

Beatrice slipped inside. The cavernous lecture hall was illuminated by a sickly, pulsing violet light. In the center of the room, standing at the professor s podium, was Elias Thorne, an embittered former professor of folklore who had been dismissed the previous year for conducting unauthorized, dangerous rituals on campus grounds.

Thorne had the Codex Noctis open before him. He was using a crude, brute-force summoning spell, slashing his own palm with a silver knife and letting the blood drip onto the ancient pages.

"Thorne! Stop!" Beatrice yelled, her voice echoing in the vast room.

Thorne snapped his head up. His eyes were wide, bloodshot, and completely unhinged. "Ah, the quiet little librarian. You re too late, Beatrice. The academic board called my theories madness. They said these creatures were just psychological metaphors. I am going to show them exactly how real a metaphor can be!"

He slammed his bloody hand down onto a sprawling illustration of a beast.

The air in the room dropped twenty degrees in a second. The ink on the page began to boil. It erupted upward, not as the delicate, controlled threads Beatrice used, but as a violent geyser of black sludge. The sludge hit the floor and began to rapidly expand, drawing in the shadows of the room, solidifying into bone, sinew, and matted fur.

It was a Gloomhound. It stood the size of a draft horse, its body entirely composed of shifting, razor-sharp obsidian scales and shadows. It had no eyes, only a gaping maw filled with rows of needle-like teeth glowing with that same violet light. It let out a sound that was less a roar and more the sound of a hundred people screaming in terror.

"Tear her apart," Thorne commanded, pointing a trembling finger at Beatrice.

The Gloomhound lunged.

Beatrice didn't panic. She fell back on the discipline of her magic. She reached into her canvas bag and pulled out a worn, leather-bound copy of Le Morte d'Arthur. She flipped to a dog-eared page, her eyes scanning the familiar text, absorbing the rhythm of the words.

"...and there came a knight of noble bearing, his armor gleaming like the sun upon the water, bearing a shield of purest silver..."

She thrust her hand forward. The black ink peeled off the page of her book in an elegant, sweeping ribbon. It spun in the air before her, expanding and hardening. Silver light flared, blindingly bright against the violet gloom.

With a clash of metal, a fully armored knight stepped out of the light, bringing a massive broadsword down directly on the Gloomhound s skull.

The impact sent a shockwave through the hall, shattering the remaining windows. The Gloomhound shrieked, falling back, its shadowy scales cracking beneath the blow. The knight a construct of pure, chivalric ideal stood silently before Beatrice, an impenetrable wall of literary steel.

"What is that?" Thorne screamed, backing away from the podium. "That's impossible! You're just a clerk!"

"I am a librarian," Beatrice corrected coldly. "And you have overdue materials."

The Gloomhound recovered quickly. It attacked the knight with a flurry of bites and shadow-strikes. The knight parried and blocked, but Beatrice could feel the strain. The Gloomhound was fueled by Thorne s lifeblood and the dark energy of the Codex; her knight was fueled only by her own mental focus. The ink-magic was draining her rapidly, giving her a pounding migraine. She needed to end this quickly.

She reached into her bag again and pulled out her second book: a collection of atmospheric Victorian ghost stories. She didn't need a specific page. She simply grasped the spine, visualized the pervasive, choking fogs of th-century London, and ripped the magical essence from the book.

A thick, pea-soup fog exploded from the book, instantly filling the lecture hall. It was so dense that Beatrice couldn't see her own hand in front of her face. It smelled of coal smoke and wet Thames mud.

Thorne began to cough violently. "I can't see! Where are you?"

Beatrice used the distraction. She dropped to her hands and knees and crawled under the swirling melee of the knight and the hound, guided by the sound of Thorne's panicked coughing and the faint violet glow of the Codex cutting through the fog.

She popped up beside the podium. Thorne swung a wild fist, but Beatrice ducked easily. She grabbed the heavy, iron-bound cover of the Codex Noctis and slammed it shut with all her might.

She pulled a heavy brass padlock from her pocket the third item she had brought and snapped it through the metal clasps of the book. As the lock clicked, the connection was severed.

Without the open book to anchor it, the dark magic collapsed. A horrific wail echoed through the fog, followed by the sound of shattering glass.

Beatrice waved her hand, releasing the magic holding the fog. As the air cleared, the lecture hall was empty of monsters. The Gloomhound had dissolved into a puddle of foul-smelling black sludge on the floorboards. Her silver knight had also vanished, leaving nothing behind but a faint dusting of dry ink on the wood.

Thorne was on his knees, weeping in exhaustion and terror, his magical reserves completely depleted.

Beatrice casually wiped a smudge of soot from her cheek, picked up the Codex Noctis, and tucked it safely under her arm. She looked down at the defeated academic.

"You failed to understand the fundamental rule of the library, Professor Thorne," she said, her voice echoing in the now-quiet hall. "Just because a book is kept in the dark, doesn't mean you have the right to read it."

She turned and walked out into the crisp Oxford morning. She had a long walk back to the Bodleian, and she needed to brew a pot of real tea before the head archivist arrived. She would have to explain the melted door, but as for the Codex, she would simply ensure it was locked away far deeper this time. And perhaps, she thought, as she summoned a tiny, ink-drawn raven to perch on her shoulder for the walk home, she would start keeping a copy of Beowulf in her desk drawer, just in case.
