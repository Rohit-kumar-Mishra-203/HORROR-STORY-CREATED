Story : The Saltwater Seamstress
The smell of brine and decaying kelp was as much a part of Morwenna as her own skin. She lived in a crooked, weather-beaten wooden shack perched precariously on the jagged black cliffs of Harrow s Point, overlooking a stretch of the Atlantic so violent it was known locally as the Devil s Jaw. Morwenna was a sea witch. While the mainland witches concerned themselves with harvests, hearths, and forests, Morwenna s domain was the churning abyss, the shifting tides, and the secrets buried in the crushing dark.

She was a solitary creature by nature and necessity. The townsfolk of the nearby fishing village of Saltcreek feared the sea, and by extension, they feared her. They believed she commanded the storms that drowned their fathers and sons. They didn't understand that Morwenna didn't command the sea; she merely listened to it, bargained with it, and occasionally, stole from it.

Her magic was woven from salt, sea glass, and the sun-bleached bones of ancient leviathans. She could sing a school of mackerel directly into a starving fisherman's nets, brew a tea of bladderwrack that cured scurvy in a single day, and, if the price was right, craft a talisman of polished abalone that would guarantee a ship safe passage through the worst nor'easter. But she rarely did the latter. The sea demanded a heavy toll for guaranteed safety, and the fishermen rarely had anything of true value to offer in exchange.

It was a Tuesday in late November, the air biting and thick with freezing drizzle, when the sea spat something unexpected onto the rocks below her shack. Morwenna had been out scavenging for ironwood driftwood when she saw it a splash of unnatural crimson against the grey slate of the rocks.

It was a man. He was tangled in the remains of a shredded canvas sail, his body battered and blue with the cold. A jagged piece of timber had pierced his side, and his breath rattled in his chest like loose stones in a tin can. By all rights, the freezing water should have killed him hours ago. The fact that he was still breathing meant the sea wasn't finished playing with him yet.

Grumbling about the disruption to her quiet morning, Morwenna hauled him up the treacherous cliff path. He was heavy, dead weight, but years of hauling soaked nets had given her a wiry, deceptive strength. She dragged him into her shack, dumping him onto a pile of seal furs near the massive cast-iron stove.

Stripping him of his sodden clothes, she examined the wound. It was deep, angry, and oozing a dark, sluggish blood. Traditional medicine wouldn't save him. Infection was already setting in, and his core temperature was dangerously low. He needed salt magic.

Morwenna worked quickly. She stoked the fire until the iron belly of the stove glowed cherry red. She crushed dried sea urchin spines into a fine powder and mixed it with a thick paste of fermented kelp and a single drop of her own blood. As she worked, she chanted in the old tongue a language that sounded like crashing waves and grinding pebbles on a shore.

She applied the poultice directly to the wound. The man screamed, his eyes flying open. They were the color of a bruised, stormy sky. He thrashed violently, trying to push her away, but Morwenna pinned his shoulders with surprising force.

"Hold still, you foolish creature," she hissed, her voice cutting through his panic like a blade. "The sea tried to claim you. I am trying to steal you back. It stings because it s working."

She forced a bitter concoction of sea buckthorn and distilled moon-water down his throat. Within minutes, the thrashing stopped. His breathing evened out, and he slipped into a deep, unnaturally still sleep.

For three days and three nights, Morwenna fought for his life. She traded secrets with the whispering winds to break his fever, and she bound his fading life force to a piece of dry driftwood using threads of spun sea-silk. If the wood rotted, he would die. If it remained dry and whole on her mantel, he would live.

On the fourth day, he woke. He was weak, but the terrifying grey pallor was gone from his skin. He looked around the clutter of the shack the drying herbs hanging from the rafters, the jars of preserved oddities, the large cauldron bubbling over the fire. Finally, his eyes landed on Morwenna, who was calmly mending a torn fishing net in the corner.

"You're the witch," he croaked, his voice raspy from disuse.

"And you're a terrible sailor," Morwenna replied without looking up. "What kind of idiot tries to run the Devil s Jaw during a full moon tide?"

"My name is Elias," he said, ignoring her insult. "I'm the captain of the Windrunner. Or... I was." A shadow passed over his face, aging him a decade in a second. "The ship?"

"Splinters," Morwenna said bluntly. "You're the only piece the ocean spat back."

Elias closed his eyes, grief washing over him. Morwenna felt a twinge of something unfamiliar in her chest pity, perhaps. She squashed it immediately. Pity was a useless emotion on the harsh coast.

"You owe me a life debt, Elias of the Windrunner," she stated, setting aside her net and standing up. "I pulled you from the deep, and I chased the rot from your blood. The sea knows it, and the sea will want payment."

Elias looked at her, his bruised eyes solemn. "I have nothing. My ship is gone, my crew is gone. I have only the clothes... well, I don't even have those."

"I don't want your gold," Morwenna scoffed. "Gold sinks. It s useless to me."

"Then what do you want?"

Morwenna looked out the salt-stained window at the churning grey water. The sea had been restless lately, angry and demanding. The storms were growing fiercer, the tides more unpredictable. She needed an anchor, someone tethered to the mortal world to help balance her wild magic.

"You will stay," she said, turning back to him. "You will chop the wood, haul the freshwater, and repair the roof that leaks when the gales blow from the east. You will tend the fires and keep the salt damp out of my herbs. You will be my hands when my magic requires my mind."

Elias stared at her, assessing the situation. He was a proud man, a captain used to giving orders from the helm, not taking them from a reclusive witch. But he was also a pragmatist. He was alive, and the woman standing before him, with her wild grey hair and eyes like polished sea glass, was the reason why.

"For how long?" he asked.

"Until the sea forgets your name," Morwenna replied. "Which, given the size of the storm you managed to survive, might take a while."

Elias slowly pushed himself up into a sitting position, wincing as his scarred side stretched. He looked around the shack once more, then looked at Morwenna.

"Very well," he said quietly. "I chop the wood."

It was a strange partnership. Elias was a creature of order and routine, while Morwenna operated on the chaotic whims of the tides and the phases of the moon. Yet, slowly, a rhythm established itself. Elias repaired the shack, making it warmer and drier than it had been in a century. He learned to identify the safe herbs from the toxic ones, and he learned when to leave Morwenna alone when the heavy magic was upon her.

In return, Morwenna taught him the ancient language of the sea. She showed him how to read the bruised clouds to predict a squall, how to find freshwater springs hidden on the rocky beaches, and how to sing the seals into a calm slumber.

The townsfolk of Saltcreek eventually noticed the smoke rising from the chimney of the witch's shack every single day, and the tall, silent man who occasionally came down to the shore to gather driftwood. Rumors flew, but fear kept them away. They decided that if the sea witch had taken a demon lover, it was best not to interfere.

Years passed. The wood bound to Elias s life force remained dry and strong, resting on the mantelpiece above the roaring stove. He never returned to the sea as a captain, but he found a different kind of command on the cliffs. He became the anchor Morwenna had needed, grounding her wild magic, while she gave him a life that the ocean had so desperately tried to steal. They were a necessary balance, standing together on the edge of the world, watching the storms roll in, completely unafraid of the dark water below.
