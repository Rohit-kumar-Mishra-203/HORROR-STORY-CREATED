Story : The Clock-Mender of Big Ben
Arthur Pendelton was a man completely ruled by routine, which was precisely why no one suspected him of being the most dangerous criminal in London.

Every evening, precisely at eight o'clock, Arthur climbed the three hundred and thirty-four limestone steps of the Elizabeth Tower. He wore a grease-stained canvas coat, carried a heavy leather satchel of iron tools, and possessed the quiet, unassuming demeanor of a lifelong civil servant. He was the Chief Horologist of the Palace of Westminster, tasked with the sacred duty of maintaining the Great Clock.

To the politicians arguing in the chambers below, and to the millions of citizens navigating the smog-choked streets of Victorian London, the clock was a symbol of the Empire s unyielding stability. To Arthur, it was a masterful, beautiful piece of camouflage.

Arthur was a chronomancer.

In the eyes of the Constabulary of Arcane Regulation (CAR), chronomancy was not merely a crime; it was an abomination. To manipulate fire or earth was to play with the paint, but to manipulate time was to tear the canvas itself. The penalty for practicing time-magic was a specialized execution: the offender was thrown into a localized temporal stasis field, frozen as a living statue for eternity.

But Arthur didn't use his magic to alter history, steal fortunes, or cheat death. He used it to keep the city running. Time was not a neat, linear progression; it was a messy, frayed rope. It snagged on historical tragedies, bunched up around moments of profound mass emotion, and occasionally, it frayed. Arthur used his magic to quietly snip the loose threads, applying temporal oil to the grinding gears of reality to ensure yesterday transitioned smoothly into tomorrow.

His silent, lifelong vigil nearly ended in catastrophe on the bitter, freezing night of December st, .

The city was in a frenzy. The dawn of a new century was hours away. Crowds were gathering along the Thames, fireworks were being primed, and the air was thick with the electric anticipation of progress.

Up in the belfry, Arthur was conducting his final inspection of the year. He stood in the deafening, rhythmic heart of the tower, watching the mesmerizing swing of the massive pendulum and the precise, mechanical ballet of the gravity escapement. The air smelled familiarly of brass polish, machine oil, and cold iron.

At exactly : PM, the rhythm broke.

It wasn't a mechanical failure. The heavy iron gears didn't slip, and the pendulum didn't stall. The break was imperceptible to normal human senses, but to a chronomancer, it was as deafening as a cannon shot.

A heavy iron wrench slipped from Arthur s workbench. It plummeted toward the wooden floorboards.

Clack. It hit the floor. Then, the wrench blurred. It flew upward, defying gravity, and landed perfectly back on the workbench. A second later, it slipped again, plummeted, hit the floor, and flew back up. Over and over. A perfect, closed five-second loop.

Arthur s blood ran cold. He spun around, his eyes frantically scanning the cavernous clock room.

He saw it hovering just above the massive striking train that controlled the Great Bell. It was a fracture in reality a jagged, glowing fissure hanging in mid-air, bleeding a silvery, luminescent mist. It looked like a cracked windowpane, but the glass was space, and the draft coming through it was pure, unadulterated temporal chaos.

Someone had tried to jump backward. A rogue warlock, or perhaps a desperate, grieving mother with access to a dark grimoire, had attempted a massive temporal leap right in the heart of London, using the symbolic weight of the turning century to power their spell. The spell had failed, but the attempt had torn the fabric of time.

The silvery mist chronal fluid was leaking into the room. Where it touched the stone walls, the mortar rapidly aged into dust, then reversed back into wet cement, endlessly looping. A moth flew into the mist, burned to ash in a hyper-accelerated lifespan, and was instantly reborn from its own dust, over and over.

Arthur pulled his pocket watch from his coat. The hands were spinning wildly, forward and backward, unable to find the true present.

He did the terrifying mathematics in his head. The Great Clock was intrinsically tied to the heartbeat of London. When the hammer struck the Great Bell at exactly midnight, the acoustic and symbolic resonance of the new century would act like a sonic boom hitting a cracked windshield. The fracture would violently shatter.

The resulting temporal anomaly would swallow the entire city. London would be trapped in a localized, permanent time loop. The millions of people celebrating outside would live the final hour of over and over again, for eternity, their minds slowly degrading to ash while their bodies repeated the same motions forever.

It was : PM. He had thirty-eight minutes to save the city.

He couldn't call the Constabulary. By the time they filed the paperwork to unseal their own strictly regulated chronal-anchors, the century would be lost. Furthermore, if they saw Arthur operating on this scale, they would freeze him the moment the crisis was averted.

He had to do it himself.

Arthur threw off his heavy canvas coat. He reached deep into his leather satchel, bypassing the mundane wrenches and oil cans, and pulled out his true tools. He retrieved a tuning fork forged from meteoric iron iron that had existed in the vacuum of space, untouched by Earth's timeline and a heavy pair of brass calipers etched with fourth-dimensional geometry.

He walked to the very edge of the glowing, chaotic fracture. The chronal radiation rolling off it was agonizing. It made his skin crawl with the phantom sensations of a thousand possible futures and a thousand past regrets.

"Hold the line," Arthur whispered to the ticking mechanical giant surrounding him.

He struck the tuning fork against the iron frame of the clock.

HUUUUUUM. The note was incredibly low, a vibration that bypassed the ears and settled directly in the teeth. Arthur closed his eyes and stepped out of his own timeline, entering the Caesura the grey, silent void between the seconds.

The deafening ticking of the clock room vanished. The pendulum froze mid-swing. The dust motes hung motionless in the air. Time was paused, but the fracture was not. Because the tear existed outside of normal time, it was still violently thrashing, the jagged edges of reality scraping against each other in a shower of silver sparks.

Arthur stepped into the mist. It was like wading through freezing molasses.

He raised the meteoric calipers. He had to physically grab the edges of the timeline and pull them together, like a surgeon stitching a wound. He jammed the heavy brass prongs into the invisible fabric of the air on either side of the glowing rift.

With a roar of sheer exertion, he squeezed the calipers.

The timeline fought back. The friction of attempting to bend reality generated immense, searing heat. The brass calipers began to glow cherry-red in his hands, blistering his palms, but he didn't let go.

"Close, damn you," he gritted out, the veins bulging in his neck.

As he pulled the edges closer, the temporal backwash hit him. Time had to go somewhere. Since he was forcing the timeline shut, the displaced chronological energy grounded itself through his body.

Arthur felt the terrifying sensation of accelerated aging. His bones ached. His lungs felt thin and papery. The thick, dark hair on his forearms turned a stark, snowy white in seconds. He was literally spending his own future life to buy the city a tomorrow.

: PM. He could feel the pressure of the true timeline pushing against his stasis spell. The Caesura was breaking down. The clock wanted to tick.

With a final, agonizing surge of strength, Arthur forced the edges of the fracture together. The silver light narrowed to a brilliant, blinding seam. But it wasn't sealed. The moment he let go of the calipers, the immense pressure of the incoming new century would blow it wide open again.

He needed a pin. A temporal anchor heavy enough to hold the seam shut forever.

Arthur reached into his vest pocket with his free hand. He pulled out a mundane, heavy brass gear a spare part for the striking train he always carried for luck. It had no magic. But it had mass.

Arthur didn't cast a spell. He made a sacrifice.

He visualized the next twenty years of his life. The quiet evenings by the fire, the books he had yet to read, the peaceful retirement he had slowly been saving for. He gathered all of that potential time, that unlived future, and violently shoved it into the brass gear.

The metal flared with a blinding, golden light, heavy with the weight of two decades of a human soul.

: PM. The stasis field shattered. The deafening TICK-TOCK of the Great Clock slammed back into Arthur's ears. The massive gears began to whir, preparing to lift the two-hundred-pound hammer for the midnight strike.

Arthur jammed the glowing, time-infused gear directly into the seam of the fracture.

He released the calipers and fell backward onto the hard wooden floor, his chest heaving, his hands raw and bleeding.

High above him, the hammer lifted.

The fracture shuddered violently. The silver mist flared, trying to reject the brass gear, trying to tear itself open to swallow the chime. The golden light of Arthur's sacrificed years fought back, acting as a metaphysical weld, fusing the torn edges of reality together.

BONG. The first strike of midnight shook the tower. The sound wave hit the repaired seam.

The seam held.

BONG. The second strike. The silver light sputtered and died. The brass gear dissolved into nothingness, completely absorbed into the fabric of the universe, leaving behind perfectly smooth, unbroken air.

BONG. The fracture was gone. The timeline was sealed.

As the Great Bell rang out the remaining nine strikes, ushering the world into the year , the muffled roar of millions of cheering Londoners drifted up from the streets below. Fireworks exploded over the Thames, painting the smog in brilliant shades of crimson and emerald.

Arthur lay on the floorboards for a long time, listening to the steady, rhythmic heartbeat of the machine he loved.

When he finally managed to push himself up, his joints popped and cracked with the stiffness of advanced age. He walked over to a small, polished brass panel on the wall and looked at his reflection.

The man staring back at him was twenty years older. Deep trenches lined his face, his eyes were clouded with the exhaustion of a long life, and his hair was the color of dirty snow. He had been thirty-five when he walked up the stairs; he was fifty-five now.

Arthur reached up and touched his face. A small, bittersweet smile touched his lips. He picked up his canvas mechanic's coat from the floor, shrugging it over his aching shoulders. He packed his meteoric tuning fork and his melted calipers back into his leather satchel, hiding them beneath the mundane wrenches and oil cans.

He had lost his youth in a matter of minutes, and no one in the world would ever know he had done it. There would be no medals, no parades, and no gratitude from the King.

But as Arthur began the long, slow descent down the three hundred and thirty-four limestone steps, listening to the pristine, unbroken flow of time ticking away above him, he knew he didn't care. The clock was ticking, the century had turned, and London was safe. The clock-mender had done his job.
