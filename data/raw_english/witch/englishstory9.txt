Story : The Bone-Singer of the Catacombs
The city of Ashbury was a gothic masterpiece of black marble, soaring spires, and perpetual, weeping rain. It was a city that celebrated its history by building directly on top of it. Beneath the cobbled streets, the rumbling carriages, and the grand cathedrals lay the true foundation of Ashbury: the deep catacombs. A subterranean labyrinth of ossuaries, forgotten crypts, and silent dust.

This was the realm of Lyra.

Lyra was a necromancer, a title that, on the surface above, would have her burned at the stake by the Arcane Constabulary. The surface dwellers believed necromancy was the art of desecration of dragging unwilling corpses from their rest to serve as mindless, shambling slaves. But true necromancy, the kind Lyra practiced, was not a magic of domination. It was a magic of profound, absolute empathy.

She was a Bone-Singer.

She lived in a repurposed vestibule deep within the fourth level of the catacombs, lit by the gentle, blue glow of foxfire moss. Lyra was pale, her skin rarely touching sunlight, and her fingers were calloused from handling rough stone and brittle bone. Her most prized possession was not a dark grimoire or a blood-stained dagger, but a silver tuning fork and a voice trained to hit the resonant frequencies of the human soul.

When a soul could not pass on tethered to the mortal plane by sudden trauma, unfulfilled vows, or agonizing regret their spiritual static agitated the surrounding bones. The catacombs would grow cold, the shadows would lengthen unnaturally, and a low, mournful weeping would echo through the limestone tunnels.

That was when Lyra would go to work.

She would walk the dark corridors, striking her tuning fork against the stone. She would listen until the pure note of the silver fractured, catching on the jagged, discordant frequency of a trapped ghost. Once she found the source a specific skull nestled in a mosaic wall, or a femur lying in the dust she would sit beside it. She would place her bare hands on the cold calcium and sing.

She didn't sing words. She sang raw, distilled emotion. She matched the spirit's frequency, creating a harmonic bridge between the living and the dead. Through the song, the spirit would manifest, appearing as a shimmering, translucent echo of its former self. Lyra would listen to their grievances, their terrors, and their heartbreaks. She would validate their pain, and then, slowly, she would change the key of her song. She would introduce notes of peace, of closure, and of the warm, inviting void.

She would sing them to sleep.

It was exhausting, sorrowful work, but it kept the massive necropolis beneath Ashbury from rising up and tearing the city above to pieces.

The sanctity of Lyra s quiet world was violently shattered on a Tuesday night in November.

It didn't begin with a phantom's wail, but with the brutal, jarring sound of shattering stone. Lyra bolted upright from her cot, her heart hammering against her ribs. The heavy, stagnant air of the catacombs was suddenly choked with the scent of pulverized limestone, saltpeter, and the unmistakable, bitter tang of human greed.

The dead reacted instantly. A wave of localized terror swept through the tunnels. Thousands of resting spirits, violently jolted from their slumber, began to panic. The temperature in Lyra s chamber plummeted below freezing. Frost bloomed rapidly across the stone walls, and the ambient foxfire moss flickered and died.

"Hush," Lyra whispered instinctively, sending a wave of calming, low-frequency hums into the dark. "Hush now. I am here."

She grabbed her silver tuning fork and a heavy iron lantern, stepping out into the corridor. The spiritual static was deafening. It felt like walking through a swarm of invisible, panicked bees.

She tracked the disturbance down to the Vault of the Penitent, a sealed section of the catacombs dating back to the city's founding. It was said to house the remains of the early martyr-kings, buried with artifacts of immense, raw magical power. Lyra never went there; the spirits in the Vault were ancient, heavy, and perfectly at peace.

Lyra crept to the edge of the Vault s grand archway and peered into the gloom.

Four men stood amidst the rubble of the ancient, magically sealed door. They were rough, scarred men wearing heavy leather aprons and carrying iron crowbars, pickaxes, and lanterns that burned with harsh, mundane oil. At their center stood a man in a tailored, albeit dust-covered, wool coat. He had sharp, rat-like features and held a pulsing, brass compass that spun wildly in the magically dense air.

"The seal is broken, Mr. Rourke," one of the thugs grunted, wiping sweat from his brow with a filthy hand.

"Excellent," Rourke replied, his voice echoing disrespectfully in the sacred space. "The Scepter of the First King is in the central sarcophagus. Break it open. And take whatever jewels you find in the wall alcoves. The collector in London is paying by the pound."

They were grave robbers. But worse, they were arcane scavengers, hunting for relics that were fundamentally tied to the rest of the ancient dead.

Lyra felt a surge of cold, protective fury. She stepped out of the shadows, holding her lantern high.

"You are trespassing," her voice rang out, startlingly clear and loud in the cavernous space. "Leave this place. Now."

The thugs jumped, raising their pickaxes. Rourke turned, squinting into the gloom. When he saw Lyra a frail-looking young woman in a simple grey dress he let out a sharp, barking laugh.

"Well, well. The legends are true. A little ghoul living in the basement," Rourke sneered. "Listen to me, girl. We are heavily armed, and we have a writ of excavation from the corrupt mayor above. Go back to your crypt and pretend you didn't see us, or we'll add your bones to the walls."

To emphasize his point, one of the thugs swung his heavy iron crowbar, smashing it into a delicate mosaic of ribs and skulls lining the entryway.

The bones shattered into dust.

The reaction was catastrophic.

A sound erupted in the vault a sound that bypassed the ears and vibrated directly in the teeth and the marrow. It was the collective scream of a hundred ancient kings, martyrs, and priests, their eternal rest violently desecrated.

The oil lanterns carried by the thugs instantly blew out, plunging the vault into pitch blackness.

"What did you do?!" Rourke yelled, his bravado entirely vanishing as the temperature dropped so rapidly their breath froze into ice crystals in the air.

Lyra didn't run. She didn't cower. She stood her ground, closed her eyes, and struck her silver tuning fork against the iron handle of her lantern.

The pure note cut through the chaotic, screaming static of the angry dead. Lyra opened her mouth and began to sing.

But she did not sing a lullaby. She did not sing of peace.

For the first time in her life, Lyra sang a war dirge. She harmonized with the ancient fury of the Vault. She became a conduit for their rage, focusing the ambient, chaotic energy of the panicked spirits into a singular, directed force.

The darkness in the vault began to move.

The thugs screamed as unseen, freezing hands grabbed their ankles. The shattered pieces of bone on the floor began to rattle and leap into the air, swirling like a localized, deadly tornado. Spectral figures, glowing with a terrifying, sickly pale light, manifested from the stone walls. They didn't have weapons; they had the sheer, crushing weight of centuries of sorrow and the terrifying certainty of the grave.

"Kill her!" Rourke shrieked, blindly pointing a revolver in Lyra's direction and firing into the dark.

The bullet never reached her. A towering, spectral figure of a long-dead king stepped in front of Lyra, the lead slug passing harmlessly through his incorporeal form.

Lyra s song grew louder, deeper, vibrating the very bedrock. She didn't command the dead to attack the living's bodies; she commanded them to attack their minds.

She forced the grave robbers to feel what the dead felt.

Instantly, the four men dropped their weapons, falling to their knees. They clutched their heads, screaming in absolute, mind-shattering agony. They were suddenly overwhelmed by the sensory memory of a thousand deaths the choking terror of drowning, the burning agony of fire, the slow, agonizing fade of illness, and the crushing, eternal isolation of the coffin.

It was too much for a mortal mind to bear.

"Make it stop!" one of the thugs wailed, tearing at his own hair, completely broken by the empathetic assault. "Please, God, make it stop!"

Lyra held the note for exactly ten more seconds, ensuring the lesson was permanently seared into their psyches. Then, with a sharp intake of breath, she cut the song.

The spiritual weight vanished. The spectral figures dissolved back into the stone. The swirling bone dust settled onto the floor.

The vault was silent again, save for the pathetic, ragged sobbing of the four men on the floor.

"The dead have spoken," Lyra said softly, her voice cold as the stone beneath her feet. "They do not wish to be disturbed. Run back to the surface. And if you ever return to the dark, they will not let you leave."

Rourke didn't say a word. He scrambled backward on his hands and knees, scrambling over the rubble of the doorway, his men following in a blind, desperate panic. They abandoned their tools, their compass, and their pride, sprinting up the winding stairs toward the surface, permanently scarred by the true weight of the afterlife.

Lyra watched them go until the sound of their panicked footsteps faded entirely.

She let out a long, shaky breath, her knees trembling slightly from the sheer magical exertion. She walked over to the shattered bone mosaic. Gently, carefully, she began to gather the broken pieces.

She sat cross-legged on the cold stone floor, pulling the dust and fragments into her lap. She struck her tuning fork against the floor.

It was time to go back to work.

She began to sing again, her voice soft, gentle, and full of profound apologies. She sang a lullaby of deep earth, quiet centuries, and unbroken sleep. It would take weeks to calm the Vault of the Penitent, to stitch the spiritual wounds the grave robbers had inflicted. But Lyra wasn't going anywhere. The surface world had its laws, its progress, and its endless greed. But down here, in the dark, Lyra was the guardian of the quiet. And she would ensure that in Ashbury, the dead always had a voice.
