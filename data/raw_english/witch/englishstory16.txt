Story : The Roots of the Hollow Ash
The village of Oakhaven did not actually have any oak trees. It had pines, it had shivering birches, and it had a single, colossal Ash tree that sat dead center in the village square. The tree was a monstrosity of nature, its trunk so wide it would take ten men linking arms to encircle it, its bark a sickly, pale grey that looked uncomfortably like dead skin in the moonlight. The villagers revered it. They held their festivals beneath its sprawling, leafless branches, they swore their marriage vows against its trunk, and when they died, their ashes were mixed with the soil at its roots.

To nineteen-year-old Elara, the tree was just a source of constant, oppressive shade. She was the village baker s daughter, a girl with flour perpetually dusting her dark hair and a mind that asked too many questions for Oakhaven s liking.

Oakhaven was a closed ecosystem. No one ever left, and strangers rarely arrived. The village was guided by the Matriarch, an ancient woman named Mother Agatha. Agatha claimed she could hear the voice of the Ash, that the tree protected them from the 'blight' of the outside world. She doled out medicines, settled disputes, and led the secretive, midnight rituals that Elara and the other unmarried youths were forbidden to attend.

The horror began slowly, creeping into Elara s life like frost on a windowpane.

It started with her younger brother, Tomas. Tomas was a vibrant, loud seven-year-old who chased stray dogs and climbed the birch trees until his knees were raw. But as the autumn equinox approached, Tomas began to change. He grew lethargic. His bright green eyes clouded over with a milky film, and his skin took on the same pale, greyish hue as the Hollow Ash.

Elara s father brought Mother Agatha to the bakery. The old woman, draped in heavy black shawls that smelled of dried lavender and rotting earth, laid her gnarled, liver-spotted hands on Tomas s forehead.

"The boy is sensitive," Agatha rasped, her voice sounding like dry leaves crushing together. "The Ash is calling to him. It requires a tribute to ensure the winter is mild. He is being blessed, Elias."

"Blessed?" Elara had snapped, stepping forward, ignoring her father s warning glance. "He s freezing, Mother Agatha. He hasn't eaten in three days. He needs a real doctor from the city, not a blessing from a dead tree."

Agatha turned her milky eyes on Elara. The air in the warm bakery instantly dropped ten degrees. "You speak of the city, child. You speak of the blight. The Ash protects. The Ash provides. Do not question the roots that anchor your home."

Agatha gave Elias a small vial of thick, black liquid. "A drop on his tongue every night. He will be at peace."

Elara didn't trust the liquid. That night, after her father had gone to sleep, she crept into Tomas s room. She found the vial on his bedside table. Uncorking it, she was hit by a smell that made her stomach heave it smelled of copper, old blood, and damp, stagnant water. She poured the liquid out the window into the dirt and replaced it with dark blackberry syrup.

For a few days, Tomas seemed to improve slightly. But the village around them was changing.

Other children began to show the same symptoms. The blacksmith s daughter, the miller s twin boys. They all grew pale, silent, and drawn, staring blankly out their windows toward the village square. And as the children withered, the Hollow Ash seemed to thrive. For the first time in Elara s memory, the dead, grey branches began to bud. Small, fleshy, crimson leaves pushed their way out of the dead wood, pulsating slightly like tiny, beating hearts.

Elara knew something was deeply, fundamentally wrong. The village wasn't being protected; it was being farmed.

On the night of the full moon, Elara decided to break Oakhaven s strictest rule. She stayed awake, dressed in dark clothes, and slipped out the back window of the bakery. The village was dead silent, the cobblestones slick with a cold, unnatural dew. She moved from shadow to shadow, making her way toward the square.

A low, rhythmic chanting drifted through the cold air.

Elara pressed herself against the side of the cooper s shop, peering around the corner into the square. Dozens of villagers, including her own father, stood in a wide circle around the Hollow Ash. They were swaying, their eyes closed, chanting in a language Elara had never heard a guttural, scraping tongue that hurt her ears.

At the base of the tree stood Mother Agatha. She wasn't hunched over her walking stick anymore. She stood tall, her black shawls discarded, revealing a body that was shockingly young and pale, though her face remained that of an ancient crone.

Lying on a flat stone altar embedded in the roots of the tree was the miller s youngest boy. He was completely unconscious, his skin nearly translucent.

"The roots are thirsty," Agatha called out, her voice echoing unnaturally in the open square. "The winter comes, and the cold demands warmth. We offer the sap of our youth to the eternal wood!"

Agatha raised a curved knife, its blade forged of black glass.

Elara clamped her hands over her mouth to stop herself from screaming. Agatha didn't stab the boy. Instead, she brought the blade down on her own forearm, slicing a deep gash. But blood didn't flow. A thick, glowing green sap oozed from the witch's veins. She pressed her bleeding arm against the boy's mouth, forcing the glowing sap past his lips.

The boy convulsed violently. As Elara watched in sheer, paralyzed horror, the boy's body began to physically shrink. His skin wrinkled, his hair turned stark white, and his life force was visibly drawn out of him, flowing like a mist directly into the massive roots of the Hollow Ash.

Simultaneously, Agatha s ancient face began to smooth. The deep wrinkles vanished, the liver spots faded, and her milky eyes cleared into a sharp, predatory amber. She was draining the child's life to feed the tree, and the tree was feeding her in return. It was a symbiotic cycle of parasitic magic.

When the boy was nothing but a desiccated husk, Agatha stepped back. She looked radiant, beautiful, and utterly demonic. The crimson leaves on the Ash tree rustled loudly, though there was no wind.

"The tribute is accepted," Agatha proclaimed. "Take the husk to the catacombs."

Two men stepped forward one of them was Elara's father. His eyes were blank, completely under the witch s thrall. They picked up the boy's remains and carried him toward the dark, gaping maw of the village crypts.

Elara ran. She ran back to the bakery, scrambled through her window, and collapsed onto her floor, violently sick. Her entire life was a lie. Her father was a puppet. Her home was a feeding ground for an immortal witch and a vampiric tree.

She knew Tomas was next. He was already weak, prepped for the slaughter. She couldn't fight the whole village, and she couldn't out-magic a witch who had lived for centuries. But she was a baker. She understood heat, and she understood fire.

The next day, Elara moved with terrifying, focused purpose. She went to the apothecary under the guise of buying flour weevil poison, purchasing large quantities of sulfur and saltpeter. She snuck into the blacksmith s shed while he was entranced by his sick daughter and stole two heavy barrels of lamp oil. She hid everything beneath the floorboards of the bakery.

She waited for the next moonless night.

The village was quiet, the heavy, unnatural sleep of the thralls settling over the houses. Elara went into Tomas s room. She bundled him in heavy wool blankets, hoisted his frail body onto her back, and carried him out into the dark. She hid him in the old, abandoned root cellar at the edge of the forest, far from the square.

"Stay here, Tommy," she whispered, kissing his cold forehead. "Don't make a sound. I'm going to fix the tree."

Elara returned to the village, hauling the heavy barrels of oil and her makeshift explosives in a wooden handcart. Her muscles screamed, but adrenaline fueled her.

She reached the square. The Hollow Ash loomed in the dark, its crimson leaves pulsing with a faint, sickening bioluminescence. It felt alive, and it felt angry. As Elara approached, the massive roots shifted in the dirt, thick tendrils of wood sliding like serpents over the cobblestones.

It knows I'm here, she realized.

She didn't hesitate. She grabbed an axe from her cart and swung it with all her might into the side of the nearest oil barrel, shattering the wood. The thick, flammable liquid poured out over the massive roots. She did the same to the second barrel, kicking it so it rolled against the trunk, splashing oil high up the grey bark.

A sharp, piercing shriek split the night.

Mother Agatha stood at the edge of the square. She was furious, her amber eyes glowing in the dark, her youthful face contorted in a mask of pure, demonic rage.

"Foolish girl!" Agatha roared, raising her hands. "You think you can burn the eternal wood? The roots go deeper than the fires of hell!"

The ground beneath Elara s feet erupted. Thick, grey roots burst through the cobblestones, wrapping around her ankles like iron shackles. She fell hard, dropping her torch.

"Your brother was next, but you will do nicely," Agatha sneered, walking slowly toward her. The witch chanted, and the roots began to drag Elara toward the flat stone altar. The bark tore at her skin, but Elara fought through the pain. She reached into her pocket, pulling out the small linen pouch containing the sulfur and saltpeter mix.

"The tree is dead, Agatha!" Elara screamed, throwing the pouch directly onto the oil-soaked roots. "It's just firewood now!"

Elara grabbed the flint and steel from her belt. Agatha realized what she was doing. The witch lunged forward, her nails elongating into sharp, black talons, but she was too late.

Elara struck the flint. A single spark fell into the sulfur mix.

The explosion was deafening. The chemical fire ignited the lamp oil instantly. A massive pillar of roaring, orange flame shot up the trunk of the Hollow Ash.

The tree screamed. It wasn't the sound of cracking wood; it was a high-pitched, agonizing shriek of a living, dying thing. The roots holding Elara instantly withered, snapping into brittle ash as the fire consumed the magical life force of the tree.

Agatha was caught in the blast wave. The fire didn't just burn her clothes; it burned the stolen youth right out of her. As the tree burned, Agatha aged a century in a second. She collapsed onto the cobblestones, shrieking in agony, her skin turning grey, her body desiccating until she was nothing more than a fragile, ancient husk, identical to the children she had murdered.

The entire village woke to the inferno. The hypnotic thrall over the villagers broke the moment the tree's core caught fire. Elara s father stumbled into the square, his eyes finally clear, staring in horror at the burning monstrosity.

"Elara!" he yelled, running toward her, pulling her away from the intense heat.

"It's over, Papa," she sobbed, burying her face in his chest. "It was the tree. It was Agatha."

The Hollow Ash burned for three days and three nights. It smelled of roasting meat and sulfur. When the fire finally died, nothing was left but a massive, blackened crater in the center of the square.

The children who had been sick, including Tomas, slowly regained their color. Without the parasitic drain of the witch and the tree, the village of Oakhaven slowly woke up to a harsh, cold reality. They had been complicit in a nightmare for generations.

Elara didn't stay to see them rebuild. A week later, she packed a bag, took Tomas by the hand, and walked past the blackened crater. They walked out of Oakhaven, past the pine trees and the shivering birches, and stepped into the outside world, ready to face whatever 'blight' was out there, knowing it could never be worse than the roots they had left behind.
