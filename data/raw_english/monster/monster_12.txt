THE HOLLOW WOOD

The ecologist's notebook. Found in the car park of Thorndale Nature Reserve, September th. Handed in to the reserve's visitor centre. Unclaimed for four months. Transcribed here in full.

September st. Arrived at Thorndale for the autumn survey. Woodland habitat assessment, standard methodology, comparing this year's data with the baseline. Reserve is acres of mixed deciduous woodland, SSSI designated. No unusual features in the reserve management notes. Expected three weeks.

Set up in the reserve's field station. The building is a converted stable, solid, adequate. The reserve manager a man named Derek Oakes showed me around this morning and was helpful in the practical sense but evasive in a way I found odd. He's been managing this reserve for twelve years. He knows it well. But when I described my survey methodology grid-based transect walks, including the northern section of the reserve he said, without elaboration: "The northern section isn't really worth your time. The habitat quality is poor up there. Stick to the central and southern areas."

I looked at the aerial photographs last year. The northern section looks fine to me. Dense mature oak, good understorey. I'll judge the habitat quality when I see it.

September rd. First two days of survey going well. Central section is excellent good oak-hazel structure, strong ground flora, indicators of old woodland across most of the area. Found three brown hairstreak butterfly sightings, which is exciting. The southern section less interesting but adequate.

Noticed that the bird life is quiet in the central area compared to what I'd expect in good oak woodland in September. The silence is notable. I'll log this systematically over the coming days.

September th. Started the northern section this morning. Derek saw me heading that way and repeated his recommendation that I avoid it. I asked him to be specific about why. He said the ground was uneven and the overhead canopy created tripping hazards. These are very thin reasons to avoid acres of reserve. I thanked him and went.

The northern section is not poor habitat. I don't know what Derek was seeing in (the last formal survey that covered this area) but what I'm seeing today is extraordinary mature oaks up to years old by my estimate, dense hazel and field maple understorey, a ground flora that includes species I did not expect to find at this location. This woodland should be flagged for enhanced designation. I cannot understand why it has been classified as low priority.

The silence is more pronounced here. In four hours of systematic walking I heard eleven bird species, all at distance, none within the northern section itself. Insects minimal despite warm weather.

I found something else. In the centre of the northern section, a clearing of approximately metres diameter. The clearing is not on any map of the reserve. The trees at the clearing's edge are all of a consistent age and have growth scars that suggest they were, at some point, horizontal fallen, or bent, and then grew back toward the vertical. The pattern of the scars is consistent across all trees at the clearing edge. Something pressed them down.

The ground in the clearing is unlike the ground in the rest of the northern section. The soil is compacted to a depth I can't fully probe, but deeper than any human compaction I can account for. The soil temperature is lower than the surrounding woodland by approximately degrees Celsius. The vegetation in the clearing is sparse and consists entirely of pioneer species the kind of flora that colonises ground that has been recently cleared. In a woodland of this age this is extraordinary. The clearing has been recolonising for no more than - years.

Something was in that clearing until recently. Something of significant mass.

September th. I have been back to the clearing twice. I do not go after three in the afternoon. This is a practical decision based on daylight hours, not anything else.

I found tracks. Not on the ground the soil is too compacted for clear impressions. On the trees at the clearing's edge. Marks on the bark, at heights between two and four metres, consistent with contact from something of great size. The marks are not claw marks in the sense I am familiar with they are not scratches or gouges. They are compressions. The bark has been pressed inward, not torn away. The pattern of the impressions is regular.

I photographed everything. I'll send the photographs to a colleague who specialises in large mammal ecology. I cannot think of anything in the UK with the size and weight distribution implied by these marks.

September th. The birds have returned to the central section over the past two days. Not in the numbers I'd expect, but present. They are not present in the northern section.

Derek came to the field station this evening. He asked how the survey was going. I told him honestly: I've found evidence in the northern section of a large animal presence that I cannot identify. I told him about the clearing, the compression marks, the soil temperature.

He sat at my table and didn't say anything for a while. Then he said: "It was here for three years. It came in and left in and I never understood where it came from or where it went."

"It?" I said.

"I never saw it clearly," he said. "I saw it twice both times at distance, both times at dawn. It was large. Very large." He paused. "It didn't behave aggressively. It didn't approach the reserve boundaries. It stayed in the northern section. It seemed to use the clearing."

"Use it how?"

He thought about this. "Sleep, possibly. Or something that served the function that sleep serves. It would be in the clearing for days at a time. Not moving. And then it would be gone from the clearing and you'd hear it moving through the northern section the trees moving, things going quiet, and then it would come back to the clearing."

"Why didn't you report it?" I said.

"Report it to who?" he said. "And say what? I have an animal in my nature reserve that I can't identify, can't approach, can't photograph, that isn't doing anything except existing?" He looked at me. "I've been managing nature reserves for twenty years. I know what our large mammals look like. I know what they do. This was not any of those things."

"Is it gone?" I said.

"I think so," Derek said. "The clearing has been empty since . The birds started coming back in ." He paused. "But the soil temperature is still off. The compression marks are still fresh they don't weather at the rate they should." He looked out the field station window. "I think it's gone. I'm not completely sure."

September th. I went to the clearing this morning at first light. The air was cold and the clearing was filled with ground fog that hadn't burned off yet, and I stood at the clearing's edge and looked into the fog and I waited.

I don't know what I was waiting for. The survey instinct the patience of someone trained to stand still and observe and let the landscape reveal itself rather than imposing motion and noise on it. I waited.

The fog moved. Not in the way fog moves when wind disturbs it. In the way fog moves when something large displaces it, creating currents in the still air. The fog moved in the clearing in the patterns of something present, something of scale, moving without urgency in the centre of the space.

I stood at the clearing's edge and I watched the fog move. I was very still. My heart was very loud. And the fog currents the displaced air moved toward me, across the clearing, and stopped at the clearing's edge where I was standing, as if something had arrived at the edge and was close enough that its breath or its warmth or the air it displaced was reaching me.

I could not see it. I could feel it. The temperature at the clearing's edge dropped another two degrees in thirty seconds. Something was there, on the other side of the clearing's boundary, very close, and I could not see it through the fog.

I backed away. I walked back to the field station. I packed my survey equipment.

September th. Three days since the clearing. I'm not going back in. The survey data I have from the central and southern sections is sufficient for the report. I'll note the northern section as "survey incomplete, habitat quality assessment pending."

I sent the photographs of the compression marks to my colleague. He called me back within an hour. He said: "These aren't from any animal I can identify. Whatever made these is much larger than anything in the European fauna." He paused. "Kate, where is this?"

I told him it was a private survey and I'd send him the full data when I'd written it up.

I'm not going to send him the full data. I don't think the full data is the kind of data that should move through email systems. I don't think it's the kind of data that has an appropriate institutional destination.

I'm going to write a report that describes the central and southern sections accurately and notes the northern section as requiring further survey. And I'm going to call Derek when I get home and tell him what I found in the fog, and we will be two people who know something that they don't know what to do with.

That feels like the honest outcome of three weeks in Thorndale. The knowledge, and the not-knowing-what-to-do-with-it.

I'll write the report on Monday.
