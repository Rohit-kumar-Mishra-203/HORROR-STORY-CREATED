The nature reserve warden found the first one in April: a red deer stag, seventeen stone of muscle and bone, lying in the clearing above the Drummore burn, entirely intact. No wound, no mark, no sign of predation. Just dead. And thin not starved-thin, not the gradual thinning of illness, but thin in the specific way of something that has had its substance removed. Like a stag-shaped sack from which the contents had been taken, leaving the container intact but hollow.

He logged it as natural causes and moved on. The Cairngorm National Park had six hundred deer and they died in various ways and this was not an unusual event.

He found the second one in May. Then four in June. Then nine in July three red deer, four roe, and two that were not deer at all but a mountain hare and a large dog fox, which was where the pattern stopped being explainable as natural mortality.

He reported it to the park authority. The park authority sent a wildlife vet named Dr. Sioned Lewis who examined the most recent specimens and filed a report that used the phrase "cause of death undetermined, post-mortem presentation inconsistent with known pathological processes" and recommended further investigation.

The warden his name was Danny Cullen, forty-three, twelve years in the Cairngorms did not wait for further investigation. He had walked these hills for twelve years and he knew their usual patterns and something was wrong with the stretch of hillside between the Drummore burn and the ridge above Coire Beag in a way that was getting more wrong by the week. He had noticed things besides the dead animals. He had noticed that the usual wildlife the deer, the grouse, the hares, the foxes, the birds was moving away from that section of the hill. It was not absent; it was peripheral. It was using routes that avoided an area of approximately two square kilometres centred on the Drummore burn's upper section.

He spent three weeks walking the perimeter of that area before he went into it. He was methodical and careful and he was genuinely frightened, which he acknowledged to himself as useful information rather than weakness.

The centre of the area, when he finally entered it on a morning in August, was a place he knew a natural amphitheatre below the ridge, sheltered from the west wind, with a small lochan that held brown trout and in summer was a watering point for deer. He had sat above this lochan many times in twelve years. He knew its sounds and its smells and the quality of its light in different weathers.

The lochan's quality was wrong. Not visibly wrong the water was clear, the sedge was green, the sky was reflected in its surface as it should be. But the quality of the air around it was different in the way that certain places are different a quality of attention, of being in a place that has a centre, that is oriented, that has something at its heart that everything else in it is arranged around.

He sat above the lochan for two hours. In that two hours, no animal came near it. He heard no birds. The wind moved the sedge and the sedge moved and that was all the movement there was.

Then, from the water, something rose.

It rose without disturbing the water's surface, which was the first wrongness. Large things entering or leaving water disturbed the surface. This rose through the surface as if the surface were not there or as if the thing rising through it was part of the water itself and was simply changing its distribution. It rose to a height of approximately three metres above the lochan's surface and it was there, present, in the late August afternoon light, and Danny looked at it from his position above and felt his understanding of what he was looking at fail several times in succession.

It was not a specific shape. This was not a failure of his vision or his processing. It genuinely was not a specific shape in the sense that he had shapes for. It was a presence above the water volumetric, dimensional, present in space but its boundaries were not the defined boundaries of a creature with a skin, with edges. Its boundaries shifted. It occupied space differently from moment to moment, as if the concept of a consistent physical form was not how it operated. It was large, and it was present, and it was alive in some sense not the animal sense, not the biological sense, but the sense of something that was doing something. That had a purpose it was engaged in.

It was feeding.

He understood this without understanding the mechanism. The lochan the air above the lochan, the specific space the thing occupied was being used, and what it was being used for was consumption. Not consumption of physical material in the way predation consumed physical material. Something else. Something that used the space above the water and the quality of the light and possibly the water itself as a medium for something he had no vocabulary for.

He did not move. He did not breathe any more than he had to. He watched the thing above the lochan for twenty minutes and then it descended not back into the water, just descended, became less present, became absent by degrees, until the lochan was just a lochan again and the sedge moved in the wind.

He walked out of the area and down to the valley and drove to the nearest town and sat in a pub until he had his hands under control.

He called Dr. Lewis and he described what he had seen. She listened without comment until he was finished and then said: "Send me your specimen data again. All of them, with GPS coordinates."

She called him back three days later. "The coordinates cluster around the lochan you described," she said. "The specimens every one of them was found within half a kilometre of that lochan." She paused. "The post-mortem presentations. What I described as 'inconsistent with known pathological processes.' What that means, in plain language, is that the animals were physically intact but biologically empty. Their tissues were intact. Their organs were intact. But the biochemical processes that constitute life metabolism, cellular activity, the entire chemical framework of biological function were simply absent. As if something had removed the process without disturbing the structure." She paused. "That's not something any predator or pathogen does. That's not even something any known physical force does."

"Something is feeding on the process," Danny said. "Not on the animal. On what the animal is doing."

She was quiet for a moment. "That's one interpretation," she said carefully.

He fenced the area. The park authority objected on grounds that fencing a two-kilometre section of open mountain was impractical and aesthetically damaging and inconsistent with the access rights legislation. He argued for six weeks and eventually compromised on warning signs laminated notices mounted on posts at the area's approaches that read: "Wildlife management zone no public access. Unstable ground."

The warning signs were his, not the park authority's. He paid for them himself and put them up on a Sunday. They were inaccurate as to the reason for exclusion and accurate as to the practical effect: stay out.

The hollow animals stopped appearing after the fence and signs went up. Whether this was because the warning was working, or because the thing in the lochan had moved on, or because it was no longer finding prey in the exclusion zone and had adjusted its range, he could not determine.

He stayed in the Cairngorms. He stayed because it was his job and because he loved the hills and because the thing in the lochan had not harmed him had not noticed him, as far as he could tell, had been engaged in what it was doing without registering him as anything relevant to that engagement. He stayed because leaving felt like surrender to something that had not actually threatened him.

He checked the lochan quarterly. He checked it in the careful, attentive way of a man who knows what he's looking at and has decided that the looking is his responsibility.

The lochan is clear and cold and holds brown trout and in summer is a watering point for deer, and has been both of those things again for four years, and the area the warden fenced is now, he believes, safe to open.

He has not opened it.

Some questions require more than the passage of time before they are settled. Danny Cullen has not yet decided that four years of quiet is an answer.
