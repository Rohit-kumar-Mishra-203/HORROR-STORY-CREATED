THE BONE GARDEN

Jade Okafor moved to the village of Melling in March because the rent was cheap and the cottage had a large garden, and Jade was a botanist who needed space to grow things. She was thirty-one, coming off a difficult two years that had included a failed relationship and a redundancy, and what she needed was ground to dig in and plants to tend and the specific therapy of watching things grow that she had relied on since childhood.

The garden behind the cottage was large sixty feet by thirty and south-facing and therefore excellent for growing, and it had been, clearly, well-maintained at some point in the not-too-distant past. There were established fruit trees, a herb bed, the remains of a vegetable patch, and in the far south corner, occupying roughly a quarter of the garden's total space, something that Jade walked toward on her first morning with her hands in her pockets and her eyes on the ground, examining the soil's condition.

The far corner of the garden was different from the rest.

Different in the way of soil that has been heavily worked deeply cultivated, heavily enriched, with the specific dark tilth of ground that has been cared for with intention and over time. The surface was covered in something that was not grass and was not any cultivated plant she immediately recognised a low-growing, dark-leaved ground cover that spread across the entire corner in a dense mat, with small flowers at its centre, deep red, that were not a species she knew.

She crouched at the edge of this corner and looked at the ground cover. She took a sample of the leaves. The texture was wrong too thick, slightly waxy, with a vein structure that was asymmetrical in a way she'd never encountered in temperate-zone species. The flowers were deep enough in colour to be nearly black at their centres, and they were opening, she noticed, in the morning sun which was correct, heliotropic but they were opening toward her rather than toward the sun. She was between the sun and the flowers, crouching in the corner's shade, and the flowers were turning not toward the light but toward her.

She went inside and washed her hands. She thought about it.

Over the following week she researched the ground cover. She sent photographs to two colleagues, neither of whom could identify the species. She looked through every reference she had access to. She found nothing matching the specific combination of features the leaf structure, the vein asymmetry, the flower colour, the behaviour toward the observer.

She tended the rest of the garden. She did not touch the corner.

The corner grew. Not rapidly not in a way she could record day-to-day. But over three weeks it expanded measurably at its edges. The dark-leaved mat pushed outward from the south corner by a consistent few centimetres per week. And the flowers, which had been red, deepened further not darker in colour exactly, but deeper in another quality that she found herself reaching for the wrong vocabulary to describe. More present, was the closest she could get. More substantial.

She talked to her neighbour an elderly woman named Mrs. Briggs who had lived next door for forty years and who had, when Jade arrived, brought her a pie and a set of recycling bins and the specific warmth of a countrywoman greeting a newcomer. Mrs. Briggs came to the garden fence when Jade called to her, and looked at the corner, and her expression moved through something that she then managed into stillness.

"You've not been in that corner?" she said.

"No," Jade said. "What is it?"

Mrs. Briggs was quiet for a moment. "The family who lived in your cottage before you," she said carefully, "had a daughter. About twenty years ago. She was a child, very young, six or seven. She used to play in that corner. And then she stopped playing in it." A pause. "She wouldn't go near it. The whole family wouldn't. They kept a fence up across that corner for the last fifteen years they were in the cottage. You can see the post holes." She pointed. Jade looked. There were indeed four post holes at regular intervals, marking where a fence had divided the corner from the rest of the garden.

"What's in it?" Jade asked.

"I don't know what it is," Mrs. Briggs said. "I know it grows things. Not usual things. And I know that the things it grows, it grows from " she stopped. "I don't know exactly," she said. "I know that the previous family had a cat that went in there one autumn. In the spring the flowers came back a new colour."

Jade looked at the deep red flowers, opening toward her in the morning shade.

"The Pattersons before them," Mrs. Briggs said, "lost a dog in there. It was a spaniel. It went in one evening and they found it in the morning at the corner's edge. Not harmed. But empty, the wife said. It breathed and walked and ate but it wasn't there anymore." She paused. "They put the fence up after that."

"And before the Pattersons?"

"Before them " Mrs. Briggs looked at the corner for a long moment. "Nobody owns this cottage before the Pattersons in my memory. But the corner was always there. Always that corner, always that dark ground cover, always those flowers." She looked at Jade. "I'd put a fence up if I were you."

Jade bought fence posts the next day. She spent a morning sinking them in the post holes and running wire between them and fastening a gate at the corner's access point. She worked quickly and efficiently and did not touch the ground cover or the flowers. When the fence was up she stood on the garden side of it and looked through the wire at the corner, and the flowers looked back turned toward her in the way they always did, orienting on her rather than the sun.

She did her research. She was a botanist; the corner was a botanical problem and she treated it as one. She collected samples from outside the corner soil samples from as close to the corner's edge as she could reach through the fence without entering it. She sent the soil samples to a laboratory for analysis. The analysis came back with trace organic compounds that did not correspond to standard soil biology proteins, complex organic molecules, the chemical signatures of processes that were animal rather than vegetable.

The corner was eating things. Had been eating things for, based on the laboratory's estimate of the compound concentrations, a very long time. And was growing something from what it had eaten.

She kept the fence up. She observed the corner through the wire for six months, recording its expansion rate, logging the flower behaviour, photographing the ground cover's development. She published a paper in a specialist journal that described the species as "unidentified ground-covering plant with anomalous heliotropic behaviour and unusual soil chemistry in the immediate substrate." It was received as a botanical curiosity.

She stayed in the cottage for three years. The fence held. The corner did not breach it seemed, in fact, to have accepted the fence as a boundary, though Jade knew that the capacity to accept boundaries was not a quality of plants.

She moved away eventually, for work reasons. She left the fence in place. She wrote to the estate agent handling the cottage and mentioned, in the most neutral and informative terms she could manage, that the south corner of the garden had unusual botanical features and should not be disturbed or the fence removed.

The estate agent filed her letter with the property documentation. Whether it was read by the next tenants, she did not know.

She kept thinking about the flowers. The way they tracked her. The specific quality of their attention not the attention of plants, which do not have attention in any meaningful sense, but something else. Something that used the shape of a plant the way other things used other shapes.

She kept thinking about the cat and the dog. About what it meant to return from a place empty.

She was a scientist. She believed in data. The data was clear: the corner contained something that consumed organic material and grew something in exchange, and the something it grew was aware of being observed.

She filed this under: further research required.

She knew, honestly, that she would not be the person to conduct it.
