WHAT THE MOUNTAIN KEEPS

The mountain did not appear on any map as anything other than a mountain. It had a name Ben Mh r Dorcha, the Great Black Mountain, in the old Gaelic and a height , metres and a grid reference and a classification and all the other apparatus of official geographical documentation. What it did not have, in any official documentation, was a designation that corresponded to what the people who had lived beneath it for centuries had always known it to be.

A warning. The mountain was a warning. Not to climbers or not only to climbers. The warning was older than mountaineering. It was older, possibly, than the people who had learned to read it, who had encoded it in place names and in the specific quality of silence with which the older inhabitants of the three nearest villages responded to questions about the mountain's upper reaches.

Finn Campbell was a mountaineer of fifteen years' experience, a Scot by birth, and he had climbed Ben Mh r Dorcha twice once at twenty-two, once at twenty-seven without incident. He knew the mountain's technical characteristics: the approach routes, the weather windows, the specific challenges of the upper ridge in winter conditions. He was confident in this knowledge. He was also, on both previous ascents, aware of a quality the mountain had that he had not found on any other mountain he'd climbed in thirty years: a quality of weight. Not the physical weight of altitude and gradient but a psychological weight, a sense of the mountain as a thing that was paying attention to its own surface, aware of what moved on it. He had attributed this, on both occasions, to the mountain's character its darkness, its isolation, its scale.

He went back a third time in October, at thirty-eight, with a friend named Seun Adeyemi who was an experienced climber and who had not been on Ben Mh r Dorcha before.

They camped at the base the first night. The nearest village was Cairnbeg, three miles south, and Finn had stopped there for supplies and spoken to the woman in the village shop, who was in her seventies and whose family had been in this glen for as long as anyone's memory ran. She had recognised him remembered him from previous visits. She had said, as he was leaving: "You'll not go above the second shoulder this time of year." It was not a question.

"We're hoping to reach the summit," Finn said.

She looked at him for a moment. "Don't go above the second shoulder," she said again. "Not in October. Not in November. Not until March." She turned to serve another customer and that was all she said.

They camped and slept well and set out at five the next morning. The approach was clear, the weather holding, the mountain in full autumn colour below the treeline and austere above it. They made good time to the first shoulder by eight o'clock. The second shoulder was visible above a broad saddle that led to the upper ridge and eventually to the summit. On the second shoulder the wind was coming from the northwest and the cloud base was low and the mountain, from the second shoulder, had a very different character from the accessible lower slopes.

"There's something up there," Seun said. He said it without drama, looking at the upper ridge with the assessing expression of an experienced climber reading conditions.

"What kind of something?"

"I don't know," Seun said. "Something that isn't weather. The birds." He gestured. The mountain's slopes below the second shoulder had the birds that mountain slopes have pipits, wheatears, a pair of ravens working the updrafts. Above the second shoulder there was nothing. Not a single bird. The silence above them was the silence of a space evacuated by its usual occupants.

Finn thought about the woman in the village shop. He thought about the quality of weight the mountain always had. He thought about the fact that on both previous ascents he had turned back at the second shoulder once due to weather, once due to a knee problem and had never reached the upper ridge.

"We're going up," he said. Because he was a mountaineer and this was a mountain and he did not have a better reason to turn back than a feeling and an old woman's warning. He believed in feelings and he believed in old women's warnings but he believed in them as signs of conditions, not as prohibitions. He believed in conditions.

They went above the second shoulder at nine o'clock. The temperature dropped immediately more than the altitude gain accounted for. The cloud closed in within twenty minutes. They were on the upper ridge in full cloud with visibility at approximately twenty metres and wind that had intensified from the northwest. This was standard mountain weather and they were equipped for it and they moved with the care of experienced people in difficult conditions.

Finn saw it first. He had moved ten metres ahead of Seun to check the ridge line, and he stopped, and looked at the thing on the ridge above him, and his mind went through several iterations in rapid succession: a man, then not a man, then a large animal, then not that either, then something for which he had no category and for which his mind attempted to create a category and failed.

It was on the ridge, fifty metres above. In the cloud and the low visibility it was not fully resolved he was seeing it through moving cloud, in poor light, at distance. He could see scale it was large, significantly larger than a man, larger than any animal on a Scottish mountain. He could see that it was on the ridge in the way that a person is on a ridge upright, oriented, moving along the feature rather than across it. He could see that it had stopped, and that the stopping corresponded with his stopping, and that whatever it was, it was aware of him.

He did not move. He did not call to Seun. He stood on the upper ridge of Ben Mh r Dorcha at nine-forty on an October morning in cloud and he looked at the thing on the ridge above him and he felt the mountain's weight that quality of attention he had always attributed to the mountain's character with a clarity that made him understand, for the first time, that the weight had never been the mountain's.

The cloud moved. A gap opened, briefly ten seconds of better visibility in the grey. He saw it more clearly. He saw that it was looking at him. He saw that it had been on this mountain for a very long time, and that this ridge was its route as much as the climbers' route, and that the two of them were standing on the same feature from opposite sides of a boundary that was not geographic.

Then the cloud closed and it was gone. Not moved gone. Present and then not present, with the abruptness of something that had decided the encounter was complete.

Finn came back to Seun. "We're going down," he said.

Seun looked at him. "What did you see?"

"I don't know," Finn said. "We're going down."

They went down. They were below the second shoulder by eleven o'clock and in camp by noon and on the road by two. They drove south without talking about it until they were past Cairnbeg, and then Seun said: "You should tell me."

Finn told him. He told him everything he had seen in those ten seconds of visibility the scale, the posture, the fact of being seen. He told him about the birds below the second shoulder and above it. He told him about the woman in the village shop and what she had said.

Seun listened and was quiet for a long time.

"Do you think it's always there?" he said.

"I think it's been there for a very long time," Finn said. "I think the mountain is known for having it. I think the people in the valley have known for generations and have kept the knowledge the way you keep a necessary and uncomfortable truth quietly, usefully, without making a fuss."

"And climbers who go above the second shoulder in October "

"Go into its territory," Finn said. "And come back. Or they don't come back, and there are other explanations."

Seun thought about this. "You're going to keep climbing mountains."

"Yes," Finn said.

"But not that one."

"Not above the second shoulder," Finn said. "Not in October." He paused. "I understand the rule now. Understanding a rule is different from being told it."

He understood it. He did not climb Ben Mh r Dorcha again. Not because he was afraid he was, but afraid was not the reason. Because the mountain had a tenant that had been there before the mountain had a name. And some things that have been somewhere long enough earn the right not to be disturbed.
