The first sign was the dog.

Marta Janssen found her border collie Bess on the back doorstep on a Thursday morning in October, and the way she found her was wrong in the specific, irreversible way of something that cannot be unseen. Bess was on the step. Bess was not harmed in any way that a veterinarian could have identified as the cause of death: no wounds, no visible injury, no sign of poisoning. But Bess was empty. That was the word that came to Marta before she had time to find a better one, before she could apply anything more clinical or more precise. The dog on the doorstep was empty. She looked the same the same black-and-white markings, the same shape but the quality of presence that made Bess Bess was absent. As if something had removed it.

The vet who came that afternoon said: sudden cardiac event. Marta did not argue. The vet hadn't seen what Marta had seen, which was not something a vet's vocabulary had a place for.

She buried Bess in the garden and told herself it was grief making everything strange.

The second sign was the barn.

The farmhouse where Marta had lived alone for eleven years since her husband left was surrounded by farmland she no longer actively worked sixty acres of field and copse and one large stone barn that she used for storage. Three days after Bess died, she went to the barn to retrieve a tarpaulin for the woodpile. She opened the barn's side door and the smell hit her first: not a specific animal smell, not rot, not anything she could name from experience. A smell that was wrong in the way that Bess had been wrong a wrongness that registered in the body before the mind, a biological alarm that her body sounded before she had processed anything with her conscious attention.

She stood in the barn doorway for ten seconds before she heard it. A sound from the far end of the barn, behind the stacked furniture and the boxes and the general accumulated material of eleven years of solitary farm life. A sound that was wet, was the word she had. Wet and rhythmic and unhurried. The sound of something feeding without urgency, without fear, without any indication that it had registered her presence at the door.

She stepped back. She closed the door. She walked back to the farmhouse at a pace that she maintained as a walk rather than a run by a considerable act of will. She locked the farmhouse door and sat at the kitchen table and breathed until her breathing was steady.

She did not call the police. She had thought about what she would say to the police There's something in my barn feeding on something and it smells wrong and my dog died empty and she had concluded this was not a conversation she was equipped to have at eight o'clock on a Thursday morning with the specific young constable who covered her area, whose name was Barry and who was twenty-four and looked it.

She called her neighbour instead. Her neighbour was a farmer named George Holt, sixty-eight years old, who had been in this valley his entire life and whose family had been in this valley for four generations, and who had the quality rare, in Marta's experience of listening to a thing without immediately moving to explain it away.

George came in forty-five minutes. He listened to what she told him. He did not say: it was probably a fox, or you were upset about the dog, or these things happen. He said: "Has anyone been working the Ridgewood fields at night?"

"The Ridgewood?" Marta said. Ridgewood was the copse on the east edge of her property. "Not that I've seen."

"I've seen lights up there twice this week," George said. "After midnight. Thought it might be poachers. But poachers don't usually stay in one place." He looked at her. "Did your dog go near Ridgewood recently?"

Marta thought about it. Bess had been strange for four days before her death reluctant to go outside, pressing against Marta's legs, which she did as a puppy but had not done in years. She had refused, on the last two evenings, to go out for her usual run to the copse edge. "She wouldn't go near it," Marta said.

George was quiet for a moment. "My grandfather had a name for something," he said. "Something that came through this valley in the nineteen-twenties. He talked about it once when I was young only once. He called it a skinhunger. Said it wore shapes. Said it wore them until the shapes wore out, and then it found new ones."

"Wore shapes," Marta said.

"Wore them like " George seemed to be reaching for something his grandfather's vocabulary had that his own did not. "Like clothes," he said finally. "Skin clothes. The shape is the outside. The skinhunger is what's inside the shape." He paused. "He said the sign of one nearby was an animal that looked itself but wasn't. Not sick. Just wrong."

"Bess was wrong," Marta said.

"Yes," George said.

They went to the barn together. George had a shotgun, which he carried with the matter-of-fact ease of a man who had carried shotguns on this land for decades. He opened the barn door and they went in with torches and found, in the far corner behind the stacked furniture, what the skinhunger had left: three dead rabbits, arranged with an oddness that was not the arrangement of an animal's feeding and was not quite any arrangement Marta had a name for. Arranged, she thought. Deliberately. As if by something that understood arrangement without understanding its purpose.

Of the skinhunger itself there was no sign. The smell was diminished but present, and both of them breathed through their mouths and moved through the barn quickly and found nothing.

"It's moved," George said. "Still in the area. But not in here."

Over the following week, Marta and George tracked it not directly, because direct tracking of something that wore shapes required knowing which shapes to look for, and they did not know. They tracked it by its leavings: dead animals, found in the specific empty condition that Bess had been found in. Empty. Used. Discarded. Seven animals over seven days, across a range of about a mile centred on Ridgewood copse.

On the eighth day, they found a shape they recognised.

It was in the Ridgewood, at the copse's centre, in a small clearing that the old trees half-roofed against the sky. The shape was a man or had the shape of a man: standing, turned partly away from them, in dark clothing. Marta's breath stopped. Then the shape turned and she saw that it was not a man in the way that Bess had not been a dog. The shape of the face was correct. The proportions were correct. The texture of the skin, the colour of the clothing all correct. But the quality of presence that made a man a man was absent. The eyes, when they found her eyes, had nothing behind them. Not the nothing of blindness. The nothing of vacancy. Of an inside that had been borrowed and not filled.

George raised the shotgun.

The shape's reaction was wrong too fast, too fluid, nothing like the way a man moves when startled. It was across the clearing and into the trees before the sound of the shot had finished echoing, and where it had been the grass was flat and there was a smell and then nothing.

They found it two days later, three miles from the farm, in a ditch on the side of the county road. The shape of the man whoever the man had been was lying in the ditch, empty, the way Bess had been empty. Whatever had worn that shape had moved on.

Marta reported the body. She said she'd found it on her walk. The police investigation lasted three weeks and concluded that the deceased was an unidentified male, cause of death uncertain, possibly exposure. Marta did not tell them anything she knew, because what she knew was not something the investigation was equipped to use.

The skinhunger did not return to her farm. Whether it had moved on from the valley entirely, or whether it was somewhere in the surrounding landscape wearing a shape she would not recognise, she did not know. She kept Ridgewood copse locked now gate and padlock, new fence on the east side. She got a new dog, a terrier named Pip, who showed no signs of wrongness.

She talked to George occasionally about it, in the oblique way of two people who have shared an experience that is not shareable in ordinary language. They talked around the edges of it. They did not use the word skinhunger again. But they understood each other.

Some things, Marta concluded, do not go away. Some things move through a landscape and move on, and all you can do is recognize them when they come close, and be careful, and wait for them to leave. Her grandfather had probably had a word for that too. The word for the knowledge that the world contains things that wear your world's shapes without being part of your world, and that the best protection is the ability to see the wrongness when it appears.

The ability to see it, and to act. And then to live with what you've seen.
