MOTHER OF COLD WATERS

The submersible had been down for six hours when the sonar registered the first anomaly.

Dr. Priya Anand was the marine biologist on the research team aboard the RV Hadal Lancer, a vessel chartered by the Oceanographic Institute to survey a newly mapped trench system in the North Atlantic. The trench a geological feature that had not appeared on any previous survey of the area, discovered via satellite sonar mapping four months prior extended to a depth of nearly eight thousand metres in its deepest section, which placed it among the deepest ocean features outside the Pacific. The Hadal Lancer had been on station for eleven days, deploying instruments and a crewed submersible called the Bathysphere Four, which could carry two people to four thousand metres.

On the sixth dive, Priya and the submersible pilot a compact, experienced Norwegian named Lars Bergman who had made deep dives in his career and who was professionally unflappable were at , metres when the sonar registered something at , metres, directly below them, that should not have been there.

The sonar showed mass. Not geological mass geological features at this depth had a specific sonar signature that four days of diving in this trench had made familiar. This was different. This was a large mass, mobile it was moving, slowly, at the maximum depth the Bathysphere Four could reach. Moving upward.

"Lars," Priya said.

"I see it," Lars said. He said it in the way he said most things levelly, the information processed and categorised before the words came out.

"What is it?"

"I don't know," he said. "I've never seen a return that size that wasn't geological."

The sonar estimated the mass at Priya looked at the number three times. The sonar estimated the mass at something in the range of a large whale, but the return was wrong for a cetacean. Whales had specific sonar signatures. This was denser, more solid. More like the return from a rock formation. But it was moving.

They watched it on the screen. It was rising. Slowly rising at approximately half a metre per second, which was not fast, but was consistent and purposeful.

"Lars, I want to go to minimum lights and hold position," Priya said.

He did it without question. The Bathysphere Four's external lights four high-intensity LEDs that illuminated a cone of approximately ten metres ahead of the craft went to their lowest setting. The ocean outside the viewports became near-total dark.

They sat in the dimmed submersible at , metres and watched the sonar.

The mass continued to rise. At , metres it was close enough that the Bathysphere Four's sonar was resolving its shape roughly ovoid, but with projections at intervals that might have been fins or limbs. At , metres Priya saw the first bioluminescence. Not from the submersible from below. A blue-green glow, diffuse at first and then intensifying, that came from the mass itself or from something on its surface. Bioluminescent ocean life was ordinary the deep ocean ran on it. This was not ordinary bioluminescence. The scale of it was not ordinary.

At , metres it was directly below them, two hundred metres of dark water between its topmost point and their hull.

"Priya," Lars said.

"I know," she said.

"My recommendation is we ascend."

"Give me sixty seconds," she said. Because she was a marine biologist and the thing below her was the largest biological entity she had ever been in proximity to and she needed sixty seconds.

"Thirty," Lars said, which was generous given the circumstances.

She had the external cameras running. She had every instrument recording. She leaned forward to the viewport and looked down.

What she saw in those thirty seconds in the blue-green bioluminescent light from below was not complete. Thirty seconds at two hundred metres, in a dark ocean, with a single viewport, was not enough to see a thing completely. She saw portions. She saw a surface that was not skin in the sense she knew skin not the smooth surface of a whale or the scaled surface of a large fish but something more complex, more textured, more like the surface of a geological feature than of a living creature, but moving. She saw movement in the portions she could see that was the movement of something alive not the movement of water around an object but the movement of the object itself, controlled, intentional. She saw light from within, distributed through the surface, and the light was not random but patterned, and the pattern shifted as she watched in a way that was she reached for a word and could not find one was communicative. Was the pattern of something producing meaning.

Lars ascended. She did not stop him this time.

They surfaced, ran the docking procedure, came aboard. Priya went directly to her cabin and downloaded the camera footage and spent four hours reviewing it. Then she went to the ship's captain and told him what they had found. Then she called the institute's director on the satellite phone.

The institute's response was: record everything, continue the survey, we'll have a team of specialists on a fast response vessel within seventy-two hours.

The specialists arrived. There were five of them marine biologists, a sonar specialist, a deep-sea palaeontologist who said almost nothing for the first two days. They reviewed the footage. They reviewed the sonar data. They deployed additional instrument packages to the trench.

The sonar tracked the mass for another eleven days. It moved within the trench system up and down its full depth, along its length, in patterns that the sonar specialist called "purposeful" and then corrected to "systematic" and then fell silent about for a while before saying: "It's surveying. It's going over the trench the way we would go over the trench. Grid pattern, overlapping passes, depth variation. It's doing a survey."

Nobody had a satisfactory response to this.

The palaeontologist whose name was Dr. Abebe Haile, whose area of expertise was Precambrian ocean life and who had been quiet for two days spoke on the twelfth day of the specialists' presence. He said: "There are accounts. In the historical record and in the biological literature, in sources that are usually categorised as speculative. Accounts of very large organisms in the deep ocean. Not accounts from the imagination accounts from surveys, from deep-water fishermen, from the specific tradition of maritime observation that preceded scientific oceanography. These accounts have always been filed under mythology or exaggeration." He paused. "I have been looking at the sonar data for twelve days. I've been looking at the footage. I've been comparing what we're seeing against the distribution of the trench system that the satellite mapping found." He paused. "The trench is not a geological feature. Or it is a geological feature. But it's not just geological. The walls of the trench show evidence of modification at specific depths. The sonar signature of the substrate at the trench floor is inconsistent with passive geological formation." He put down his papers. "I think the trench is a home. I think what we're looking at has lived in this ocean for longer than we should be comfortable thinking about. And I think we have sailed into its home and parked on its front step and put lights on."

The ship's captain made a decision within three hours of Abebe's presentation. He moved the Hadal Lancer twenty miles from the trench coordinates. Not officially not with a formal report that described the reason accurately. Officially it was a routine repositioning for instrument maintenance.

The sonar did not pick up the mass again at the new location. The trench system was, from twenty miles distance, a feature on a map. A newly discovered geological anomaly. Significant, requiring further study.

Further study has not yet been conducted. The institute's follow-up expedition, scheduled for the following summer, was cancelled due to funding. The summer after that, a different team proposed a survey. The proposal was reviewed by a committee that included three members of the original Hadal Lancer team. The proposal was declined.

Priya Anand has published two papers from the Hadal Lancer expedition. Neither of them describes the mass at , metres. Both of them describe the trench's geological characteristics and call for further investigation of its unusual substrate properties.

She keeps the camera footage on an encrypted drive in a fireproof box in her home office. She looks at it occasionally. Not to extract new information she has extracted everything she can extract from thirty seconds of footage in a dark ocean.

She looks at it to remember what she saw in those thirty seconds. To maintain the accurate knowledge of the world's size, and to resist the comfortable tendency to scale the world back down to something that fits in the space between what she knows and what she doesn't.

It doesn't fit. The world is larger. She keeps looking at the footage to remember this.
