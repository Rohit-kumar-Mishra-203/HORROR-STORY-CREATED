THE KEEPER OF THE BONE COAST

The coast between Dunmarra Point and the Seal Rocks was called the Bone Coast by the fishermen who worked that stretch, and had been called so for as long as any fisherman's family could remember, and the name was partly for the white chalk cliffs that gave the shoreline its bleached, skeletal appearance and partly for the wrecks. The wrecks were the coast's defining characteristic: eight ships in the last two centuries, broken on the rocks in storms of varying severity, some of which had been severe enough to account for them and some of which had not. The worst wreck on the Bone Coast had happened on a calm June night in , with no storm and clear visibility, when the trawler Maid of Inverness had run onto the Seal Rocks and gone down in forty minutes, with the loss of six crew.

The sea on the Bone Coast was unpredictable in a way that the fishermen who worked it understood as personality. Not a threatening personality, exactly more the personality of something that had its own concerns and was not primarily oriented toward human navigation. The sea on the Bone Coast did what it did and the fishermen accommodated this by being careful, by not going out when the signs were wrong, and by the practice that had been maintained along this stretch of coast for at least three generations: the keeping of the Bone Light.

The Bone Light was not a lighthouse. It was a fire a large fire, coal and driftwood, maintained in an iron basket on the cliff top above Dunmarra Point, lit from October to March on any night when the weather was uncertain or the sea was running high. It was maintained by the current keeper, a man named Robert Cullen, seventy-one, who had taken over the responsibility from his father, who had taken it from his, who had received it from the community practice that had existed before any specific family kept it. Robert lit the fire when the conditions required it, and he kept it through the night, and in the morning he doused it. He had done this for forty years.

The Bone Light was not for the ships. This was the thing that outsiders did not understand, when they encountered it and enquired about it. It was not a navigational aid in the conventional sense. It was not sending a signal to ships at sea. It was sending a signal to what was in the sea below the Bone Coast to what lived in the deep water off Dunmarra Point, in the specific trench that the local charts marked as "unexplored" and that the fishermen called the Keeper's Hole.

The Keeper's Hole was the deep place. It was where the something lived that had lived off the Bone Coast since before the fishermen had fished it, since before the first village had stood on the Dunmarra headland, since Robert's father had put it this way since the coast was made.

The something in the Keeper's Hole was not malign toward the fishermen. Robert would say this clearly when anyone asked, because the clarity mattered. It was not malign. It had its own character, its own purposes, its own relationship with the sea it lived in and the coast it lived below. But it was enormous, and when it moved it disturbed the water in ways that the sea's surface expressed differently from weather the specific short, choppy churn of disturbed deep water coming up, the changes in current that set a small boat's compass spinning. And when it was disturbed itself when something in its environment alarmed or irritated it, when the night was wrong in ways that Robert had learned to read from forty years on this cliff it came close to the surface. And when it came close to the surface, the sea on the Bone Coast became the specific deadly sea that had put eight ships on the Seal Rocks.

The Bone Light was for the Keeper the thing in the Keeper's Hole not for the ships. The fire on the cliff top was a signal: someone is watching, someone is present, we are here and attending. Whether the Keeper understood this in the way a conscious being understood signals, Robert could not say. He had not seen it clearly. He had seen the surface disturbance that indicated its presence below, the specific optical distortion of a very large submerged mass against the refracted light of a night sky. He had felt what his father had called its mood in the way the sea communicated it, in the specific quality of the wave action and the current and the sound the water made against the cliff base. He had learned to read these things. He had learned that when the fire was lit and maintained, the quality of the Keeper's mood was different from when it was not. Quieter. Less disturbed. As if the fire said something that mattered.

On a Thursday in November, in Robert's forty-first year of keeping the light, the fire failed.

He had lit it at dusk as always the conditions were rough, a weather front from the northwest bringing squalls, the kind of night where the light was necessary. He had fed it at nine o'clock and at midnight and at two, and at three o'clock when he went to feed it again the iron basket was cold. Not guttered cold, as if the fire had not been lit. As if forty years of keeping fires in this basket had never happened.

He stood at the cliff edge with no fire behind him and looked at the sea.

The sea was moving wrong. The surface chop was the specific short, disordered chop that he associated with deep disturbance with the Keeper moving. He had not seen it this pronounced in a long time. He had not seen it this pronounced since a night in when a boat had come too close to the Keeper's Hole in bad conditions and had lost its engine and had been picked up by the Dunmarra lifeboat with a crew that was physically unharmed and would not speak of what they had seen in the water before the lifeboat reached them.

He had nothing to light the fire with. The basket was cold inexplicably, impossibly cold, as if the cold from the sea was extending up the cliff face. He had no kindling, no lighter. He stood in the November dark on the cliff top above Dunmarra Point and he did the only other thing he had.

He shouted into the wind, toward the sea, toward the Keeper's Hole below. He shouted words that were not special words not ancient words, not ritual words. His own words, the words of a man who had been keeping the light on this cliff for forty years and who understood that the signal was the point and that the signal could be delivered in other ways if the fire was gone.

"I'm here," he shouted. "I'm watching. We are here. We are attending."

He shouted it four times. The wind took it. He did not know what the Keeper heard, or whether it heard in the way that implied ears and language and comprehension. He knew that the fire worked that forty years of fire had made a difference and he knew only one other thing he could do in the absence of the fire, which was to be present with his whole attention in the way that the fire expressed presence, and to shout into the dark what the fire had always said silently.

The sea did not calm immediately. But after twenty minutes of his shouting and his presence on the cliff, the surface chop changed quality. The specific disordered churn of deep disturbance eased. The sea became a rough sea again the ordinary rough sea of a November squall, with weather behind it, consistent in its motion.

He stood on the cliff until dawn. In the morning he went to the iron basket and found it not cold but warm the warmth of a fire recently doused, of banked coals still retaining heat. As if the fire had never gone out. As if the cold had been a temporary thing, a momentary interruption, and the fire had relit itself while he was shouting into the dark.

He fed the fire with new coal and let it burn through the morning and doused it at noon when the squall had passed.

He told nobody about the cold fire and the shouting. He wrote it in his notebook the detailed notebook of forty years of keeping the light with the care of someone who understands that the record matters even when no one will read it in time for it to make a practical difference. He wrote what happened and what he did and what the result appeared to be.

He continued keeping the light. He would keep it until he could not, and then he would find someone to keep it after him, because the keeping was the point and the point was not his alone to maintain. The Bone Coast had been the Bone Coast for longer than his family had been keeping the fire, and the Keeper had been in the Keeper's Hole longer than the coast had had a name.

Robert lit the fire. He kept the covenant.

The sea continued to be the specific sea that it was unpredictable, vast, occasionally dangerous, and home to something very old that had its own relationship with the coast above it. The coast accommodated this. The fishermen accommodated this. The fire on the cliff top accommodated this.

And the something in the deep water, on the nights when the fire burned amber against the November sky, was as nearly as Robert could read it accommodated in return.

He thought that was enough. He thought it was, actually, quite a lot.
