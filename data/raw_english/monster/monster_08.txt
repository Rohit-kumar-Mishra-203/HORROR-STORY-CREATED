THE SOUND THAT HUNTS

The acoustic research station on Shetland had been operational for twelve years, and Dr. Yusuf Osman had been its director for the last eight. The station monitored ocean sound the broad-spectrum acoustic environment of the North Atlantic, from the infrasound frequencies of geological activity to the ultrasonic ranges of marine mammal communication. It was, in the global network of ocean acoustic monitoring, a significant node: the North Atlantic was acoustically complex, and the station's position gave it coverage of a substantial portion of the north-east Atlantic basin.

Yusuf had spent eight years listening to the ocean. He had developed, in that time, an intimate knowledge of its acoustic character the specific sound signatures of individual whale populations, the particular acoustic fingerprints of geological features, the seasonal variation in the ocean's background hiss that corresponded to temperature, salinity, and traffic. He knew what the North Atlantic sounded like. He knew its moods, its rhythms, its specific language of physics and biology.

In February of his eighth year, something new appeared in the data.

He found it during a routine review of the previous week's recordings the methodical weekly audit that he conducted himself because he believed that familiarity with the data at this level of granularity was the director's job, not the graduate students'. He was listening with headphones to a file from the previous Tuesday at : AM and he heard something that he went back to immediately, that he listened to three times before he took off the headphones and sat back in his chair.

The sound was he reached for categories. It was not biological, in the sense that he knew every biological sound source in the North Atlantic and this matched none of them. It was not geological geological sounds had specific signatures. It was not technological shipping, industrial activity, military sonar had all specific identifiable characters. It was none of these. It was a sound in the range of to Hz infrasound, below the threshold of human hearing but within the range the station's equipment captured and it was complex. Not the simple waveform of geological activity, not the complex but patterned waveform of cetacean communication. Something else. Something that used the infrasound range with a complexity that implied the word came before he could stop it intention.

He ran frequency analysis. He ran spectral analysis. He ran every standard analytical protocol and several non-standard ones. The sound had internal structure. The sound had repetition. The sound had variation that was not random. He was looking at data that had all the characteristics of a communication signal and none of the characteristics of any communication source he had documentation for.

He called a colleague in Bergen. She listened to a file he sent her and called back within two hours. "Where did this come from?" she said.

"My Tuesday recording," he said. "Eighteen to twenty-three Hz. I've been going through the archive and I'm finding it irregularly since about October. Always at night. Always from the same bearing roughly south-southwest, consistent with a source somewhere in the mid-Atlantic."

"It's not geological," she said.

"It's not biological. Not any biology I know."

A pause. "Yusuf, the repetition structure. Have you run a cross-correlation against the Tuesday recording and this one from " she named a specific date.

He had not. He ran it as she waited. The cross-correlation was positive the two recordings, three weeks apart, shared structural elements. Not identical. Related. Like two sentences in the same language.

"Something is out there," she said. Not as a revelation. As a statement of what the data showed.

"Yes," he said.

"Something large enough to produce infrasound at this intensity from mid-Atlantic range," she said. "And using it in a structured way."

"Yes," he said.

They spent the next six weeks building a picture. They pulled recordings from the mid-Atlantic ridge acoustic monitoring network, from NOAA's hydrophone array, from three other monitoring stations that had geographic coverage of the relevant area. They found the sound in all of them, on various dates going back eighteen months. They triangulated source locations not precise, given the nature of infrasound propagation in the ocean, but approximate. The source was mobile. Over eighteen months it had moved significantly through the mid-Atlantic, with a range that covered an area thousands of kilometres across.

"This is one thing," Yusuf said, late on a Friday evening, looking at the accumulated data. "One source, moving."

"Or a coordinated group of sources," his colleague said.

"The internal structure doesn't support a group. Groups produce harmonic relationships between members. This is coherent. It's one thing."

He looked at the intensity calculations. "One thing large enough to produce infrasound at these levels at this range has to be " he stopped, because the size implied by the physics was not a size that a living thing was supposed to be. "Very large," he said.

The NOAA researchers who reviewed his report were cautious in their language and extensive in their alternative hypotheses. Geological venting was considered and rejected. An undiscovered population of large cetaceans was considered and not rejected, but the specific acoustic characteristics made it a poor fit and they knew it. The report was filed with a designation that placed it in the category of "anomalous acoustic events requiring further investigation" the scientific equivalent of a holding pattern.

Yusuf continued monitoring. The sound appeared in his data thirty-one more times over the following two years. He became an expert in its specific characteristics could identify it within the first few seconds, the way you identify a familiar voice. He tracked it as well as the station's equipment allowed.

On the twenty-ninth occurrence, the sound did something new. It changed. The internal structure shifted a subset of the repetition pattern modified, became something he had not heard before. He spent three weeks analyzing this before he said to himself, quietly, in the empty monitoring room: it knows the station is here. It knows something is listening.

He did not say this to anyone else. He wrote it in his private notebook. He thought about what it meant to produce a modified signal in the direction of a source that had been listening to you what that action represented, what it communicated.

He thought it represented acknowledgment. The thing in the North Atlantic had found his station in its acoustic environment, had identified it as a listener, and had adjusted its signal accordingly.

He sent a signal back. Not words he had no language for it. A sound, in the same frequency range, with a structure he designed to indicate: I am here, I am listening, I received your modification.

He never heard a direct response. The sound appeared four more times over the next six months and then stopped. He monitored for two years after the last recording and did not hear it again. Whether it had moved out of range, or had changed its frequency, or had concluded the exchange, he could not determine.

He published three papers. None of them used the word "monster" or any word that implied anything about the nature of the source beyond: large, mobile, acoustically complex, biologically unidentified. The papers were correct and careful and incomplete in precisely the ways that maintained his professional standing.

In his private notebook he wrote: I do not know what it was. I know it listened. I know it heard me listening. I know it told me something I cannot decode. And I know that somewhere in the North Atlantic, in the infrasound frequencies below the range of human hearing, the ocean is not empty in the way I was taught it was empty. There is something in it that has been in it for long enough to learn how sound moves through water. Long enough to use that knowledge deliberately. Long enough to find a listening station and send a message toward it and receive an answer and respond.

Long enough, perhaps, to have been in that ocean when the ocean was young, and to have watched everything that happened to it since.

He thought about this for the rest of his career. He did not stop thinking about it.
