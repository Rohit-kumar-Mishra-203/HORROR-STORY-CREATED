THE DEEP ROAD

The tunnel had been part of the B road system for eighty years, bored through the Welsh hillside in as a wartime necessity and maintained since then with the minimum investment that kept it technically safe. It was four hundred metres long, single-lane, with a passing place at the halfway point. Its walls were rough concrete, its lighting was intermittent, and the drivers who used it regularly had an unwritten agreement that the person closest to the entrance had right of way and that everyone would keep their speed down and their lights on and their time in the tunnel short.

The unwritten rule that nobody discussed but everyone who drove the B regularly knew the one that was not about passing etiquette or speed was about the halfway point.

You did not stop at the halfway point.

If you met another car at the halfway point if you arrived at the passing place and a car was already there, waiting you did not stop to chat, did not exchange pleasantries, did not take longer than necessary. You pulled in, let the other car pass, and drove on. You did not linger. You did not stop your engine. You did not, under any circumstances, get out of your car at the halfway point.

The Hendersons did not know this rule because they were from Nottingham and were driving through Wales on a holiday and had turned onto the B by mistake following a GPS instruction that had misread the road numbers.

There were four of them: Paul, forty; Laura, thirty-eight; their daughter Kira, fourteen; and their son Max, nine. They entered the tunnel at four-fifteen in the afternoon on a Wednesday in July, which was fine the tunnel was manageable in daylight hours, and there was nothing in the character of the afternoon to suggest that this drive through the B would become the story that the Henderson family would tell for the rest of their lives with increasing difficulty, the story getting harder to tell the further they moved from it in time, not because the memory faded but because the memory did not fade and they did not know what to do with that.

At the halfway point, the car stopped.

Not braked stopped. The engine did not stall. The engine was running, idling perfectly, and the car simply ceased to move, as if something had taken hold of it from beneath and was holding it motionless. Paul pressed the accelerator. The engine responded with sound but not with forward movement. He tried reverse. Same result.

"Dad?" Kira said.

"It's fine," Paul said, which was the thing fathers say.

He got out. He walked around the car. He crouched to look at the wheels, which looked unobstructed, which looked normal. He stood up and stood in the halfway point of the tunnel and heard the sound that the tunnel made the low, resonant acoustic of a cylindrical space, amplified and specific and then heard something underneath the acoustic that was not the tunnel. Something that was using the tunnel's resonance as a medium. A sound very low and very sustained, moving through the concrete and the rock around the concrete and the space between him and the tunnel walls, and the sound was directional. It came from beneath the road surface.

He looked at the road surface. The concrete was original eighty years of surface, cracked at the edges, patched in three places, with the specific greying of decades of traffic. He looked at the patches. The patches in the concrete were not patch-shaped. They were irregular, and their edges did not correspond to any cracking pattern he could identify. They were, he thought, the specific shape of a closed hand. Or of a closed hand's impression, pressed up through the concrete from below, forced up through the material by something on the other side trying to push through.

The sound intensified. The tunnel walls vibrated he could feel the vibration in his feet, through the soles of his shoes, through the road surface. And the patches in the concrete he watched them they moved. Very slightly. The concrete flexed, or did not flex but gave, in the specific way of a surface pushed from below by something pressing upward against a ceiling.

Paul ran back to the car. He did not fully decide to run; his body made the decision.

He got in and shut the door and pressed the accelerator and this time the car moved. It moved with a violence as if released rather than driven and it shot forward and covered the remaining two hundred metres and came out of the tunnel into the July afternoon light with a speed that was more than Paul had intended.

He pulled over on the road's shoulder and stopped the car. His hands were shaking. In the back seat, Kira and Max were completely silent the silence of children who have understood that whatever just happened was beyond the vocabulary their parents had given them for frightening things.

"Paul," Laura said. She said it very quietly. She was looking in the wing mirror.

He looked in the wing mirror. The tunnel entrance was visible the dark oval of it, a hundred metres behind them on the road. As he watched, from the tunnel entrance from the road surface at the tunnel's mouth a hand emerged.

Not a human hand. The scale was wrong too large, the fingers too long and too many, and the colour of it was the colour of the concrete it was coming through, grey-white and rough, as if the material of the road had shaped it. It emerged to the wrist, the fingers splayed on the road surface, and then it was still. Testing. Registering. And then it withdrew, slowly, back through the road surface, and the road surface was smooth and ordinary again.

Paul drove. He drove for twenty miles without stopping, and then he pulled into a petrol station and sat for a while.

Nobody in the car spoke for a long time.

Laura was the one who spoke first. She said, with the careful controlled voice of a woman managing something very large: "I want to understand what that was."

"I don't know what that was," Paul said.

"But you saw it."

"And the hand."

"So we all saw the same thing."

In the back seat, Kira said: "Dad, what lives under roads?"

Paul did not have an answer for this. He had never needed an answer for this. He drove them to the holiday cottage they had booked in Pembrokeshire and they spent a week there and they went to beaches and ate good food and did the things you do on a Welsh holiday, and they did not talk about the tunnel.

But they drove home a different route, adding forty-five minutes to the journey, and when Max, three months later, asked if they could go back to Wales, Paul said yes and then planned the trip and planned the route and planned it so that they would never, under any circumstances, be anywhere near the B .

He found, afterward, that he had a new habit: looking at road surfaces when he stopped at traffic lights, at junctions, when he parked. Looking at the texture of the tarmac, the quality of the concrete. Looking for patches that were the wrong shape. Looking for signs of pressure from below.

He found this habit distressing and could not stop it. It was the knowledge that would not leave the knowledge that road surfaces are ceilings as well as floors, and that what is beneath them is not only cable and pipe and the structured underworld of civil engineering. That there are things under roads that are not in the engineer's plans. That some roads are lids on things, and some lids are imperfect, and the thing you cannot un-know once you know it is: you have been driving over lids your entire life.
