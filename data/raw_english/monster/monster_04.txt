THE WEIGHT BENEATH THE ICE

The Antarctic ice core research station was eighty kilometres from the coast and three thousand metres above sea level and the most isolated place Dr. Helen Voss had ever worked, which was saying something because she had spent fifteen years working in isolated places. She was a glaciologist. She studied ice. She had worked in Greenland, in the Canadian Arctic, in Svalbard. She knew isolation. She knew the specific quality of a place where the world was reduced to a few people and a great deal of white.

The station was staffed by six people over the southern winter: herself, two other scientists, and three support staff. The research was ice core extraction and analysis pulling columns of ice from depths of up to three thousand metres and reading in them the atmospheric record of hundreds of thousands of years. It was the kind of work that gave you a deeply specific relationship with time. Ice cores made time legible. They made deep time thousands, tens of thousands of years physically present, holdable in your hands.

What they found in the deepest cores was the first sign.

The cores at , metres came from ice that was approximately , years old. Standard ice core material, anticipated, consistent with what the geological models predicted. But the cores at this depth contained, in the samples from two specific dates in their extraction sequence, a biological trace that did not belong in , -year-old Antarctic ice.

Not a contaminant they were meticulous about contamination. The trace was in the ice itself, in the ice that had been sealed from the surface for eight hundred millennia. The trace was cellular material biological cells, recognisable under the microscope as cells because they had cell walls and internal structures, but not recognisable as any specific organism or cell type in their reference databases. They were alive. This was the extraordinary thing they were metabolically active at the temperatures of the Antarctic ice, which was below the known limit for biological activity. They were alive at temperatures at which nothing should be alive.

Helen's colleague a microbiologist named Dr. Sam Park who had been brought along specifically for the biological component of the core analysis spent three weeks working with the cells before he said what he had been thinking for most of those three weeks: "These aren't cells from an organism that lived , years ago and were preserved. The metabolic activity is current. Something is alive down there. Something is producing these cells, currently, from somewhere at or below the depth we're extracting from."

Helen looked at the data. She looked at the cells under the microscope moving, actively, in ways that cells at these temperatures should not move. "Something is alive in the ice," she said.

"At , metres," Sam said. "Yes."

"What kind of something?"

"I can't place the cell type," he said. "I can't identify any organism in my reference material that matches the morphology. The closest analogue " he paused " the closest analogue is cells from organisms that live in extreme environments. Hydrothermal vents. Deep ocean sediment. Life that operates outside the conditions we consider basic requirements." He paused again. "But this isn't analogous. This is something else. Something for which we don't have a reference."

They drilled deeper. The cells became more numerous more concentrated as they went lower. At , metres the concentration was dense enough that the cores, examined under the scope, appeared to be partially composed of biological material. The ice at this depth was not purely ice. It was ice interpenetrated with something living.

At , metres, the drill stopped.

The drill did not malfunction. The power output was normal, the rotation was normal, the pressure was within operational parameters. The drill simply stopped advancing. As if something below it was holding it in place.

Helen and the drilling engineer a man named Pieter who had eighteen years on Antarctic drilling rigs spent two days trying to understand why the drill had stopped. The ice at the depth of the halt showed no mechanical reason for resistance no rock inclusion, no density anomaly, nothing that should have stopped the drill. The drill simply could not go further.

On the second day of attempting to restart the drill, Pieter said: "It's not the ice that's stopping it."

"What do you mean?" Helen said.

"The resistance is variable," he said. "It changes. Mechanical resistance doesn't change rock or dense ice is the same resistance every time you press against it. This changes. It's more resistance when I increase speed. Less when I slow down. It's " he stopped. He looked at the drill head data on his screen. "It's responding," he said. "It's responding to the drill."

Helen thought about the cells at , metres. About something alive in the ice. About the concentration increasing with depth. About what was at , metres, holding the drill.

"Stop the drill," she said.

They stopped the drill. They extracted it. They sealed the borehole.

Helen spent the rest of the winter at the station writing up the cell data and the geological data and the drilling cessation data. She wrote it accurately and completely. She sent the full report to the institute. The institute's response was measured and careful and concluded that further investigation was required.

She recommended against further investigation of the , -metre depth.

She was asked to explain this recommendation. She wrote: "The cellular material at this depth is from a living organism or organisms of unknown nature currently active at that depth. The drilling cessation at , metres is consistent with the hypothesis that this organism or organisms responded to the drilling activity and acted to prevent its continuation. I recommend that we do not continue drilling in this location, as we do not have any basis for understanding what we would be disturbing, and we have preliminary evidence that what is there is capable of response and possibly of objection."

The institute reviewed this recommendation and declined to endorse it and indicated that a follow-up expedition would be mounted the following season.

Helen was not on the follow-up expedition. She had moved to a different research position. She received, by email, a brief summary of the follow-up expedition's findings seven months later. The borehole had been reopened. The drill had descended to , metres. Below , metres, the drill had ceased to function entirely not simply ceased to advance but stopped operating. Electrical failure, the report described it as. The drill's electronics had simply stopped working at , metres and had resumed working when the drill was raised above that depth. No explanation had been found for the electrical failure.

The expedition had not attempted further drilling.

The full report from the follow-up expedition included, in an appendix, a note from the expedition's microbiologist. The note said that the biological cells from the new cores at , metres were similar to Helen's original samples but showed a significant change: the cells' metabolic activity was increased compared to the original samples. Not marginally. By an order of magnitude. As if, in the year between Helen's expedition and the follow-up, whatever produced those cells had become more active.

Helen read this and sat very still for a while and then filed the email and went back to her current work, which was in Greenland and was eight hundred kilometres from the nearest thing like what was in the Antarctic ice, and which she was grateful for.

She thought about deep time about the , years of ice above whatever was down there. About what had been alive in that ice for , years. About what it had been doing in the dark beneath the Antarctic plateau while the surface world went through eight cycles of glaciation, while humanity evolved and spread and built cities and discovered science and drilled holes in the ice.

She thought about what being disturbed by a drill might mean to something that had been there for , years and had managed, until recently, to be entirely unmolested.

She thought that the electrical failure in the drill's electronics was probably the most diplomatic possible response.

She thought, too often for comfort, about what a less diplomatic response would look like.
