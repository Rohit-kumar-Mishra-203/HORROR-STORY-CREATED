THE CELLAR THING

They called it the cellar thing because it lived in the cellar of the Moorside farmhouse, and because it had no other name that they had ever discovered, and because after two generations of coexistence the family had developed the specific pragmatism of people who share a space with something they cannot fully categorise.

The Barlow family had farmed the Moorside land for a hundred and forty years. Great-grandfather William had built the farmhouse in , and in the course of digging the cellar had found the cellar thing. Or the cellar thing had found William. The family accounts varied on the causality of this encounter; they agreed on the outcome, which was that great-grandfather William had emerged from the cellar with a specific modification in his attitude toward the farmhouse and toward certain practices that the family had maintained ever since.

The practices were these: The cellar door was kept shut but not locked. The cellar was never entered after dark. A specific portion of the farm's produce described in William's original account as "tithe," in a usage that the family acknowledged was archaic but which had never been replaced by a better word was left at the top of the cellar stairs on the first Monday of each month. And the family did not discuss the cellar thing with anyone outside the family. The neighbour, the doctor, the vicar, the schoolteacher none of these were told what lived beneath the farmhouse floor. The knowledge was kept close, kept managed, kept from generating the kind of attention that would disturb an arrangement that was, in practical terms, working.

Sandra Barlow was the fourth generation. She was fifty-four, a farmer of great competence and limited patience, who had inherited the farm from her father and with it the knowledge of what was in the cellar and what the practices required. She had received this knowledge at sixteen the age the Barlows had traditionally used for the telling and had responded to it with the practical seriousness of a girl who had been raised on a working farm and who understood that some things were conditions of the land and required management, not opinions.

She managed it well. The tithe was left on the first Monday. The cellar door stayed shut. Nobody went down after dark.

The arrangement was disturbed in the April of Sandra's fifty-fourth year by her daughter-in-law, who was twenty-six, who had married Sandra's son James two years previously, and who was named Katie and who was bright and curious and who noticed that the cellar door was always shut and that the farmhouse's otherwise functional family never used the cellar for anything, and who asked about it in the direct, reasonable way of someone who does not yet know that some questions are the kind that, once asked, cannot be unasked.

Sandra told her. She sat Katie down at the kitchen table and told her the family history and the practices and the reason for them, because the other option continuing to offer inadequate explanations that Katie's intelligence would eventually expose as inadequate was less responsible than the truth.

Katie listened with the specific, still attention of someone processing a large amount of new information. When Sandra was finished, Katie said: "Can I see it?"

"No," Sandra said.

"Has anyone in the family seen it recently?"

"My father. My grandfather. William's account."

"What do they describe?"

Sandra looked at her daughter-in-law. "Large," she said. "Very old. It's been in that cellar since before the house. Possibly since before the hill the house sits on, though that's family speculation. It's in there and it has been in there and it doesn't come out and we don't go in and the arrangement works."

"Does it eat the tithe?" Katie asked.

"Something happens to it," Sandra said. "In the morning it's gone."

Katie thought about this. "It seems " she paused " not unfair. As arrangements go."

"It's not unfair," Sandra said. "It was William's view that it was more than fair that the arrangement was far more to the family's benefit than to the thing's. He described it as a restraint on its part. That it could have been otherwise, and it chose this instead."

"What does that mean?"

Sandra looked at the cellar door the ordinary wooden door in the kitchen wall that was always shut. "It means," she said, "that what's in there is capable of more than it does. And it chooses to do less. And we honour that choice with the tithe and the practices, and we don't test the choice."

"And if we did test it?"

"William wrote one thing very specifically in his account," Sandra said. "He wrote: 'Do not test what has chosen restraint. Restraint chosen is not restraint compelled. What restrains itself can unrestrain itself.'"

Katie nodded slowly. She sat with this for a while. Then she said: "I want to leave the tithe this Monday. If that's all right."

Sandra looked at her. "That's the family's responsibility," she said.

"I'm family," Katie said.

She was. Sandra said yes.

Katie left the tithe on the Monday the specific items, the specific amount, the specific placement at the top of the stairs that the family records described. In the morning it was gone, as it always was. She reported this to Sandra with the composure of someone who had made a choice and was comfortable with having made it.

She told James that night. He already knew the telling had always been at sixteen, but Sandra had spoken to James when she spoke to Katie, because Katie's questions had brought it to a head and James needed to know what his wife now knew. James responded with the specific combination of relief and residual unease of someone who has carried a childhood secret and has now shared it with the person they're closest to.

"Does it bother you?" he asked.

"Yes," Katie said honestly. "But the wrong things bother me less than the alternatives would bother me. I'd rather know."

James thought about the cellar door the door he had grown up not opening, not as a rule imposed by his mother but as a thing that simply was, that the house contained along with all the other things the house contained. "My grandmother used to say it was a privilege," he said. "Living alongside something like that and not being harmed by it. She said most people don't get the chance to know the world is that big."

Katie thought about this. She thought about William and his tithe and his practices and the hundred and forty years of careful, quiet management that had produced a farm on a hill in Yorkshire where a family lived and worked and a cellar contained something ancient and vast and restrained, and the two coexisted because someone, a long time ago, had been sensible enough to recognise that coexistence was an option and to build it carefully.

"It is a privilege," she said. "A strange one."

"The best privileges usually are," James said.

They left the tithe every first Monday. They always would.
