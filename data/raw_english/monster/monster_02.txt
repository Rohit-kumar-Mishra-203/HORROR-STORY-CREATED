THE OLD MEAT

The abattoir at Rixton had been closed for twenty years when the county council contracted the demolition company to take it down. The site had planning permission for residential development and the demolition was the first step a straightforward commercial demolition of a two-storey brick structure built in , decommissioned in , and maintained in a state of minimal holding since then, which meant the structure was standing, the electricity was off, and nobody had been inside in the better part of a decade.

The demolition crew was four people: a site manager named Steve Parrish, two demolition operatives named Will and Darren, and a health and safety assessor named Katherine Lowe who came in for the first day to sign off on the pre-demolition structural assessment.

Katherine did the structural walk-through first thing on the Monday morning, ahead of the crew's heavy work, because she liked to understand a building before it came down. She had done this job for eleven years and she had a specific facility for understanding structures for reading what a building was made of and how it had been used and what it would do when stressed. She walked through the abattoir's ground floor with a torch and a clipboard, making notes, checking the structural integrity of the walls, assessing the floor for load-bearing capacity.

The ground floor was straightforward: the main processing hall, the cold rooms (long since switched off, their insulation degraded), the entry areas, the administration. Standard s industrial construction. Solid. Demolition-ready.

The sub-level was different.

Katherine had the plans. The plans showed a basement level: refrigeration plant housing, maintenance access, drainage system. Standard for an abattoir of this period. She descended via the concrete stairs, which were sound, into the basement.

The refrigeration plant was as expected. The drainage system was as expected. The maintenance access tunnels were as expected, though they were tighter than she liked and she made a note that the crew would need to take care in this area.

The fourth room she entered was not on the plans.

She checked the plans twice. The room accessed via a connecting door from the main drainage gallery did not appear on any of the architectural plans she had. It was not on the original plans. It was not on the refurbishment plans. It was not on any document in her possession. It was a room of approximately ten metres by eight, with brick walls, a concrete floor, and a single overhead socket long since disconnected that suggested it had at some point had lighting.

The room was cold. Not the ambient cold of a basement that had not been heated in a decade. The specific cold of a space that was generating its own cold an active cold, lower than the surrounding basement by several degrees.

The room was not empty.

Whatever was in it was in the far corner or occupied the far corner in the way that things that are present in a space occupy a specific part of it. Katherine could not see it clearly in the torchlight. She could see mass large, low, in the corner, not shaped like any storage material she had encountered in twenty years of walking through industrial spaces. She could see movement the mass moved, a very slow rhythmic movement, something between breathing and the motion of a large sleeping animal.

She stood at the door for forty seconds. Then she backed out, closed the door, and walked back through the drainage gallery and up the stairs and out of the building.

She found Steve in the site cabin, on the phone, who took one look at her face and ended his call.

"There's something in the basement," she said.

"Something what kind of something?"

"Not a person. Not an animal I recognise." She sat down because her legs wanted her to. "There's a room not on the plans. Something is in the room. It's alive."

Steve was a practical man with twenty-two years in demolition, which meant he had encountered most of what old buildings contained, which was usually asbestos and dry rot and the occasional stolen car in an adjacent field. He had not encountered this. But he had the practical instinct to take Katherine's face seriously.

He called the council. The council was confused. He called the structural engineer who had signed off on the original plans. The structural engineer was retired and could not be reached immediately. He called the health and safety department, because Katherine was his H S assessor and she was telling him there was an undocumented room with something alive in it, and H S was the only official channel he could think of.

The H S department sent two people the following morning who descended to the basement and confirmed the undocumented room and came back up fifteen minutes later and made phone calls. The phone calls, over the following two days, produced a sequence of officials from agencies that Steve had not previously encountered in twenty-two years of commercial demolition, culminating in a woman from what she described only as "a regulatory body that doesn't have a public name" who arrived on the Wednesday with two colleagues and who spoke to Steve and Katherine separately and at some length.

She told them, in plain language, that the room was known. That the building had not been decommissioned randomly it had been decommissioned because the activity of the abattoir had sustained whatever was in the room, and when the abattoir closed, the room's occupant had required managed decommissioning, which she described with the professional brevity of someone who had done this before.

"Managed decommissioning," Steve said.

"It needed to be in a state where it could persist without the activity that had sustained it," the woman said. "The cold room arrangement the existing refrigeration infrastructure was adapted to provide the conditions it required. The arrangement has been running since ."

"What is it?" Katherine asked.

The woman was quiet for a moment. "Very old," she said. "Older than the abattoir. It was there when the abattoir was built, and the architects of the structure were aware of it, and built around it, and informed the council at the time. The council made the same decision they've made at every point since: manage, don't disturb."

"Twenty years," Steve said. "It's been in there twenty years since the abattoir closed, with nobody going in."

"It's persistent," the woman said. "It doesn't require interaction. It requires conditions. The conditions have been maintained via the utility connection that you'll see on the billing records as 'site maintenance' it's the power to keep the baseline cold room temperature at the level the arrangement requires."

"You've been paying electricity for twenty years to keep a monster comfortable," Katherine said. She did not mean to say it exactly like that. The word monster came out because it was the only word she had.

The woman looked at her steadily. "We've been maintaining an arrangement for twenty years that prevents a very old thing from being disturbed," she said. "Disturbed things respond. We prefer that it doesn't."

The demolition was postponed indefinitely. The planning permission for residential development was quietly revoked on grounds that Steve never found officially documented. The abattoir building stood. The electricity kept running to the basement. The council's annual accounts continued to show "Rixton industrial site maintenance" as a line item of modest but consistent cost.

Katherine moved to a different county. She continued her work. She walked through old buildings with a specificity she had not had before a new quality of attention to the rooms that were not on the plans, to the cold that had no radiator, to the door in the wall that opened onto space that should not be there.

She found two more in eleven years. She reported both of them to the same regulatory body. The same processes were initiated. The arrangements were maintained.

She thought about what the woman had said: disturbed things respond. She thought about it every time she descended into an old basement, every time she opened a door onto an unexplained space. She thought about what responding meant, from something very old that had been managing its existence in the sub-floor of an industrial building for a century.

She thought that responding probably meant something significant. She thought that avoiding the response was probably worth the cost of the electricity.
